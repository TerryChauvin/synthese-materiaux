# Green Synthesis of N,S-Doped Carbon Dots from the Giloy Stem for Fluorimetry Detection of 4-Nitrophenol, Triple-Mode Detection of Congo Red, and Antioxidant Applications
## Abstract
AbstractSynthesis of doped carbon dots (CDs) with enhanced fluorescence properties from green precursors has gained considerable attention due to their multipotential applications such as chemical as well as biosensing, photocatalysis, electrocatalysis, drug delivery, and bioimaging applications. Here, we have prepared highly fluorescent N,S-doped CDs (N,S-CDs) using a single natural precursor, the Giloy stem, by a hydrothermal method to explore their multipotential applications. These N,S-CDs were demonstrated as fluorescent probes for the selective detection of toxic organic pollutants that include 4-nitrophenol (4-NP) and congo red (CR) with limits of detection (LODs) of 380 and 62 nM, respectively. The fluorescence quenching observed in N,S-CDs was the result of the inner filter effect (IFE). The methods developed were used to detect 4-NP and CR in real samples, such as tap water and pond water, with recovery ranges from 93.88 to 103.45% and from 95.05 to 99.04%, respectively. Furthermore, these N,S-CDs were employed for colorimetric and smartphone-assisted detection of CR. The advantages of using a smartphone for the detection of analytes include naked-eye visualization of the probe–analyte interactions, the use of a portable and easy-to-handle device, and the elimination of the need for an expensive spectrophotometer. In addition to the sensing application, the N,S-CDs showed radical scavenging activity.
## Introduction
The fabrication of luminescent carbon dots (CDs) has been tremendously explored due to their easy fabrication method, adjustable size, excellent luminescence properties, strong resistance to photobleaching, biocompatibility, and multifunctionalities., The exceptional characteristics of CDs open numerous applicabilities in diverse fields such as biomedicals, optoelectronics, catalysts, and chemical sensing.− Heteroatom-doped carbon dots have garnered significant attention due to their enhanced optical properties, high quantum yield, increased stability, and the presence of a wide range of new functional groups. Nitrogen (N) and sulfur (S) are frequently used as dopants for CDs due to the similar size of N with carbon atoms and the achievable incorporation of these atoms into the carbon matrix. The strong combination of the N atom with the carbon atom can increase the fluorescence quantum yield, whereas the S atom can modify the density state and create new emissive trap states. Therefore, simultaneous doping of N and S can more effectively tune the fluorescence properties of CDs by inducing a red shift in the maximum absorption wavelength in addition to the enhanced fluorescence intensity. Substantial progress has been made in the synthesis of N- and/or S-doped CDs., Among various synthetic methods, the hydrothermal method using natural biomass has become the most commonly used strategy for the synthesis of CDs. In this direction, different natural resources such as mushrooms, garlic, green tea leaves, and spinach have been utilized as carbon and nitrogen sources for the fabrication of nitrogen-doped CDs.− However, the synthesis of N,S-doped CDs generally requires an additional chemical reagent, with a natural precursor or a combination of different biomass. Very few reports on the hydrothermal synthesis of N,S-doped carbon dots utilizing a single natural precursor without any additional surface passivating agent are available., Recently, we also reported a green synthetic hydrothermal route for the synthesis of N,S-CDs using a single natural precursor, Gandha Prasarini leaves, and applied it for the sensing of the food colorant tartrazine and antibacterial activity. Hence, the synthesis of highly luminescent N,S-doped carbon dots using a natural precursor via a hydrothermal method is desirable to explore their multifaceted applications.
The rapid industrialization and unsustainable growth strategies across the world adversely affect human health and the environment. The continuous release of nonbiodegradable organic pollutants such as dyes, pesticides, and food additives without proper treatment is the major cause of water pollution that affects the food chain and ultimately human beings., 4-Nitrophenol is extensively used in pharmaceuticals, pesticides, manufacture of dyes, fine chemicals, and explosives. Long-term exposure to 4-NP can strongly affect the central nervous system, skin, and vagal nerve endings. The other side effects include headache, drowsiness, cyanosis, and nausea upon accumulation of 4-NP in the human body. Therefore, 4-NP is categorized as a hazardous environmental pollutant by the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency. The maximum allowable concentration of 4-nitrophenol (4-NP) in drinking water is 0.43 μM. In addition, organic dyes like congo red can cause serious problems to the environment and health due to its low biodegradability and complex aromatic structure. CR is an anionic diazo synthetic dye, used in textiles, food, paper, and pharmaceutical industries. The metabolic product of CR is benzidine, a carcinogen, capable of causing cancer and mutagenesis in living beings. As nonbiodegradable organic contaminants stay in the environment for extended periods, a sustainable method for detecting 4-NP and CR is essential from a toxicity perspective. Although various techniques are used for the detection of 4-NP− and CR,− fluorescence sensing is preferred due to its simplicity, high selectivity, and accuracy. In contrast to traditionally used fluorophores (semiconductor quantum dots, metal–organic frameworks, and metal nanoparticles), the CD-based fluorophore has unique advantages such as low toxicity, chemical inertness, photochemical stability, and biocompatibility. In this regard, CDs are explored as fluorescent probes for the detection of 4-NP, and CR, with high selectivity and sensitivity. Although the fast-response, selective, and sensitive fluorometric methods have been explored for the determination of organic pollutants, the development of a simple, instrumental, and directly visualized mini portable device for the detection of organic pollutants remains a challenging task.
Herein, N,S-CDs are synthesized by simple hydrothermal treatment of the medicinally important Giloy (Tinospora cordifolia) stem. The variety of terpenoids, alkaloids, lignans, and steroids present in the Giloy stem serves as the source of C, N, and S and also as the passivating agent for the formation of N,S-CDs. This methodology is less time-consuming and cost-effective, making it more eco-friendly by utilizing the Giloy stem as the precursor and water as the solvent for the one-step synthesis of N,S-CDs. The synthesized N,S-CDs, which have a quantum yield of 7.2%, were investigated as a fluorescent probe for the sensitive and selective detection of organic pollutants 4-NP and CR. The multiple organic pollutant detection is achieved by a single N,S-CD-based platform that saves both labor and cost. In line with our recent interest in the development of the CD-based multimode sensing platform, the synthesized N,S-CDs are further explored as a smartphone-enabled colorimetric probe for the detection of CR. This smartphone-based colorimetric method facilitates the visual detection of CR through the naked eye using an easy-to-handle, mini portable smartphone device. In addition, these N,S-CDs show antioxidant activity.
## Experimental Section
## Materials and Reagents
Giloy stems were collected from the area near MSCB University in Baripada, Odisha, India, and were washed with Millipore water for further use. Congo red, Mg(NO3)2, ZnCl2, Cd(NO3)2, MnCl2, Cu(NO3)2, NaNO2, Na(NO3)2, BaCl2, KI, KCl, 4-nitrophenol, 4-aminophenol (4-AP), 2-nitrophenol (2-NP), 3-nitrophenol (3-NP), phenol, sucrose, dextrose, P-benzoquinone (P-BQ), P-phenylenediamine (P-PDA), 1-chloro-3-nitrobenzene (CNB), 1-naphthol, aniline, methylene blue (MB), malachite green (MG), NaCl, and 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) were purchased from SRL Chemicals, India. All chemicals used were of analytical grade with a purity greater than 99% and were utilized without any further purification. A dialysis membrane with a molecular weight cutoff of 1000 Da and a filter membrane with a pore size of 0.22 μm were sourced from Himedia Chemicals in India. Milli-Q water (18.3 MΩ) was used for the preparation of all solutions.
## Preparation of N,S-CDs
The N,S-CDs were synthesized by a hydrothermal method using the Giloy (T. cordifolia) stem. Initially, 2.0 g of stems were thoroughly washed with Millipore water, crushed with a mortar and pestle, and then soaked in Millipore water for 24 h. The extract was poured into a Teflon-lined autoclave and heated at 160 °C for 5 h. The resulting solution was cooled to room temperature before being filtered and centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 20 min to eliminate larger particles. Subsequently, the solution was filtered through a 0.22 μm cellulose nitrate filter membrane and purified by dialyzing in a dialysis bag (MWCO = 1000 Da) against deionized water for 48 h. The solution was kept at 4 °C for further analysis.
## Characterization
The morphology and size of N,S-CDs were investigated using a transmission electron microscope (Tecnai G2 20 Twin-FEI with a 200 kV operating voltage). The ultraviolet–visible absorption spectra were obtained by a UV–vis spectrophotometer (UV-2450, Shimadzu, Japan). The fluorescence spectra were observed by a fluorescence spectrophotometer (RF-6000, Shimadzu, Japan). The fluorescence lifetime measurements were done by the IBH 5000F Nano LED equipment from Horiba Jobin Yvon (Edison, NJ). The Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectrum was verified by using a Bruker Fourier transform infrared α-II spectrophotometer. The X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) studies were recorded by an Omicron NanoTechnology spectrometer with Al–Kα irradiation. The X-ray diffraction (XRD) spectra were acquired from an X-ray powder diffractometer (PANalyticalX’Pert PRO) source with Cu Kα and a wavelength of 1.54 Å. The ζ-potential of the N,S-CDs was assessed by dynamic light scattering (Nano ZS 90, Malvern, U.K.).

## Detection of 4-Nitrophenol and Congo Red by the Fluorescence Method
Different concentrations of 4-NP and CR solutions were prepared with milli-Q water, and subsequently, 1 mL of each solution was mixed with 2 mL of N,S-CDs (2 μg mL–1). After 1 min, the fluorescence spectra of solutions were observed using 430 nm excitation. The interference of other chemicals found in industrial wastewater along with the above pollutants was investigated individually. The spectra were also taken in different time variations to find the optimal condition as well as the credibility of N,S-CDs as probes for detection.
## Detection of Congo Red by the Colorimetric Method
For the colorimetric detection, different concentrations of congo red (0.1–60 μM) were added with 2 mL of N,S-CDs and after 1 min of incubation, the absorbance of the samples was taken in a UV–vis spectrophotometer.
## Detection of Congo Red by the Smartphone Method
2.0 mL of N,S-CDs and 1.0 mL of congo red with different concentrations from 0 to 100 μM were added, and the mixture was allowed to stand for 1 min at room temperature in a glass vial. The sample was then placed in a self-made black box (37.5 × 29.5 × 24.5 cm3) under UV light. The solutions were placed under UV light of 254 nm and photos were captured from a preset distance of 10 cm by a OnePlus 10R smartphone with a 50 MP rear camera with the “color-grab” application and converted into R, G, and B parameters.
## Preparation of Real Water Samples
For preparation of real water samples, water was collected from Rani Bandha located nearer to MSCB University, Baripada, Odisha, India. The pond water and tap water samples were filtered through 0.45 μm of the cellulose nitrate filter paper to remove the suspension and large particles. The filtered solutions were spiked with 4-NP and CR at different concentrations separately, and their recoveries were observed.

## Results and Discussion
## Synthesis and Characterization of the N,S-CDs
The N,S-CDs were synthesized by hydrothermal treatment of the Giloy stem as a green precursor (Scheme 1).
The N,S-CDs are formed through a process of copolymerization, aromatization, and carbonization, where the chemical constituents of the Giloy stem facilitate the doping of N and S and formation of the carbon lattice., The structural morphology of the synthesized N,S-CDs was studied with transmission electron microscopy (TEM). The TEM image (Figure 1a) shows spherical particles with a uniform size range from 3 to 7 nm. The hydrodynamic size of the N,S-CDs was also studied by dynamic light scattering (DLS) and found to be 68.74 nm (Figure S2). The XRD pattern of N,S-CDs (Figure 1b) shows a broad peak near 23° corresponding to the (120) plane and suggests the amorphous nature of N,S-CDs.,
The doping of N and S in the CD matrix and the presence of these functional groups were also validated by XPS analysis. In a typical survey spectrum of the N,S-CDs (Figure S1, Supporting Information(SI)), the peaks at 162, 280–290, 399, and 520–540 eV were ascribed to the binding energy in the S 2p, C 1s, N 1s, and O 1s regions, respectively. The percentages of C 1s, O 1s, N 1s, and S 2p were found to be 83.02, 12.74, 3.99, and 0.26%, respectively. The high-resolution scan for C 1s was well fitted into five peaks. The C 1s spectrum centered at 284.4 (C–H/C–C,C=C), 284.9 (C–N/C–S/C–OH/C–O–C), 287.5 (C=O), 292.2 (COOH/COSH), and 294.9 eV (π–π* transition), respectively (Figure 2a). The high-resolution N 1s (Figure 2b) shows peaks at 398.4, 399.4, and 401.1 eV, which corresponds to the C–N–C, N-(C)3, and N–H bonds, respectively. The two deconvoluted peaks of high-resolution O 1s at 530.4 and 531.4 eV can be ascribed to the presence of the CO group and C–O, respectively (Figure 2d). High-resolution S 2p appeared as a doublet that can be deconvoluted into four subpeaks, which represents two for each S 2p3/2 and S 2p1/2 (Figure 2c). The lower binding energy components of both the deconvoluted peaks correspond to the −C–S– covalent bond of thiophene/thiazine, while the components at a higher binding energy correspond to oxidized sulfur S=O bonding. The FT-IR study was also done to determine the functional groups present in N,S-CDs (Figure S1b,SI). The broad peak at 2900–3250 cm–1 corresponds to −NH2/–OH groups. The peaks at 1670, 1574, and 1413 cm–1 suggested the presence of C=O, N–H, and C–N vibration bands, respectively. The peaks at 1080 and 685 cm–1 were ascribed to the C=S vibrations and the presence of C–S groups, respectively.
## Optical Properties of N,S-CDs
The optical properties of N,S-CDs were thoroughly investigated through UV–vis and fluorescence spectroscopy, showcasing their potential applications in various fields. The UV–visible absorption, emission, and fluorescence excitation spectra are presented in Figure 3a. N,S-CDs show a broad UV absorption at 277 nm and a prominent shoulder peak at 338 nm, which correspond to the π–π* transition of C=C and the n-π* transition of the C=N/C=O group, respectively. The aqueous solution of N,S-CDs was yellow in daylight and turned to intense green under UV excitation (254 nm) (Figure 3b). The N,S-CDs show the usual excitation-dependent fluorescence (Figure 3c) due to the existence of surface-emissive traps and different sizes of N,S-CDs. On varying the excitation wavelength from 360 to 500 nm, there is a gradual red shift of the emission peak from 458 to 564 nm. The strong emission wavelength at 520 nm was obtained upon an excitation wavelength of 430 nm. The three-dimensional (3D) diagram of fluorescence spectra is shown in Figure 3d. The quantum yield of the N,S-CDs was calculated to be 7.2% with reference to quinine sulfate. The photostability of N,S-CDs was studied under different conditions like pH, ionic strength, and UV irradiation to determine their practical usability. The synthesized N,S-CD solution has pH 5.6. As shown in Figure S2a, there was no noticeable change in the fluorescence intensities of N,S-CDs from pH 4–6 but there were significant changes in fluorescence intensities after pH 6 toward basic conditions. In high ionic strength (1 M NaCl) (Figure S3b, SI) and under UV irradiation (100 min) (Figure S3c, SI), there was no substantial change in the emission intensity. The measurement of the fluorescence intensity for 90 days indicated that the synthesized N,S-CDs had excellent photostability (Figure S3d, SI).
## Fluorescence Sensing of 4-Nitrophenol
Due to the abundance of different polar functional groups in N,S-CDs, we investigated the possibility of using such carbon dots for detecting strongly polar and environmentally toxic molecules like 4-nitrophenol. The fluorescence emission spectra of N,S-CDs are illustrated in Figure S4a, comparing the absence and presence of 4-NP. The N,S-CDs exhibit a strong fluorescence at 430 nm, and 4-NP does not show any fluorescence. On addition of 4-NP with N,S-CDs, the fluorescence intensity of N,S-CDs progressively decreases, indicating that 4-NP effectively quenches the N,S-CDs. Considering the affinity between N,S-CDs and 4-NP, the fluorescence probe was investigated with varying concentrations of 4-NP, as illustrated in Figure 4a. The fluorescence intensity of N,S-CDs progressively decreases with the addition of 4-NP from 0.5 to 230 μM. A good linear relationship was found in the concentration range from 0.5 to 6 μM and from 0.5 to 230 μM of 4-NP and the R2 values for both linear equations (Figure 4c,d) were 0.997 and 0.993, respectively. The linear equations for 0.5–6 and 10–230 μM were y = (0.00215 ± 5.08 × 10–5) × x + (1.6993 ± 1.72 × 10–4) and y = (0.0126 ± 1.82 × 10–4) × x + (1.4898 ± 0.0217), respectively. The limit of detection (LOD) for 4-NP was calculated based on 3σ/k, where σ is the standard deviation of the regression response and k is the slope of the linear plot found to be 380 nM from the linear range of 0.5–6 μM. The limit of quantification (LOQ) was calculated based on 10σ/k, where σ is the standard deviation of the regression response and k is the slope of the linear plot and found to be 1.28 μM.
In addition to sensitivity, selectivity is another crucial parameter for evaluating the performance of a sensing system. We tested the fluorescence intensity change in the presence of different interfering aromatic compounds such as 4-AP, 2-NP, 3-NP, phenol, 1-naphthol, P-BQ, P-PDA, CNB, and biomolecules like sucrose and dextrose. There was no significant change in the emission intensity except for 3-NP, which showed very little change in the emission intensity. The resonance stability and steric effects likely account for the high selectivity of 4-NP. To our delight, the as-synthesized N,S-CDs can easily distinguish between 4-NP and its isomers. The response time for 4-NP was studied and is given in Figure S5. With the addition of 4-NP, the emission intensity for N,S-CDs decreased immediately. The N,S-CDs showed good selectivity, sensitivity, and quick response time for the detection of 4-NP. The fluorescence response of N,S-CDs for 4-NP was compared with the previous results (Table S1, SI). It showed that the N,S-CD-based sensing platform offers a rapid and facile detection process and has the potential for the sensing of 4-NP in practical samples.
## Possible Mechanism
To investigate the quenching mechanism, the absorbance spectra of 4-NP were taken, and its spectral overlap with the fluorescence excitation and emission spectra of the N,S-CDs were observed. As shown in Figure 5a, the excitation spectra of N,S-CDs partially overlap with the absorbance spectra of 4-NP, which suggests that the fluorescence quenching may be due to IFE or FRET.
But the FRET mechanism is distance-dependent and the distance between the fluorophore and the absorber should be less. The ζ-potentials of both N,S-CDs (−10.7) and 4-NP (−19.6) (Figure S6a,b, SI) are negative, and so there is Coulombic repulsion in between them. So, FRET might not have occurred as the distance between them increased because of repulsion. To strengthen our understanding of the mechanism, we conducted a thorough investigation of the lifetime of N,S-CDs, comparing their behavior in the presence and absence of 4-NP (Figure 5b). The lifetimes of N,S-CDs with and without 4-NP were 3.93 and 3.84 ns, respectively. This result confirmed that the quenching mechanism for the emission intensity of N,S-CDs with the addition of 4-NP was due to IFE. The quenching mechanism was also described by using the Stern–Volmer equation (eq 3)
where I0 and I are the fluorescence intensities of N,S-CDs in the absence and presence of 4-NP, [Q] is the 4-NP concentration, and Ksv is the Stern–Volmer constant. When we drew I0/I versus [Q] (Figure S5, SI), the data demonstrated a linear relationship within the concentration range for 4-NP (0–230 μM). The bimolecular quenching constant was calculated by using the equation Ksv = kqτ, where kq is the bimolecular quenching constant, and τ is the average lifetime of N,S-CDs. The value for Ksv was observed to be 1.2 × 104 L mol–1 from the plot, and the average lifetime was calculated to be 3.93 ns, resulting in a kq value of 3.1 × 1012 L mol–1 s–1. This observed value is greater than the maximum value possible for dynamic quenching, which is 2 × 1010 L mol–1 s–1. Therefore, it can be concluded that the interactions between the N,S-CDs and 4-NP were likely static in nature and may involve the formation of a ground-state complex.

## Fluorescence Sensing of Congo Red
The abundance of polar functional groups in the synthesized N,S-CDs at pH 5.6 motivates us toward the detection of polar groups containing toxic industrial pollutants like congo red, which remain in the ionic form at this pH. CR was often used in various industries such as textile, paper, dye, food, cosmetics, etc. The staining process of CR requires a lot of water, thus contaminating water and causing water pollution. Investigations into the degradation of CR revealed that its complex aromatic structure and stability contribute to its resistance to both biodegradation and photodegradation. Therefore, it is necessary to quantify the amount of CR added to the products accurately to decrease the hazardous effect on human health and the environment. So, it is essential to study the working conditions, sensitivity, selectivity, and response time of the synthesized N,S-CDs to develop the precise sensor for CR. The fluorescence intensity of N,S-CDs excited upon 430 nm decreased significantly with the addition of 60 μM CR. Figure 6a shows the gradual decrease in the emission intensity with the addition of CR from 0–60 μM. CR is an anionic dye with the pKa value of 4.1, and it has two negatively charged SO3 functional groups in its molecular structure. Therefore, at and above pH 5, CR produces polar groups (R-SO3). The synthesized N,S-CD solution has pH 5.6, which may be due to the presence of a number of carboxyl functional groups. So the addition of CR to N,S-CDs resulted in the formation of polar groups. This facilitates the interaction between N,S-CDs and CR, resulting in a decrease in the emission intensity of N,S-CDs. A good linear relationship was found with R2 = 0.997 and 0.993 in the regions of 0.01–1 and 1–50 μM, respectively, with the regression equation given in Figure 6c,d. The linear equations for 0.01–1 and 1–50 μM were y = (0.0751 ± 0.0016) × x + (1.403 ± 7.78 × 10–4) and y = (0.03339 ± 0.0011) × x + (1.403 ± 0.0328), respectively. The limit of detection (LOD) is calculated based on 3σ/k, where σ is the standard deviation of the regression response and k is the slope of the linear plot found to be 62 nM from a linear range of 0.01–1 μM. The limit of quantitation (LOQ) was calculated based on 10σ/k, where σ is the standard deviation of the regression response and k is the slope of the linear plot found to be 208 nM.
The interference of ions (Ba2+, Ca2+, Cd2+, Cu2+, Mg2+, Mn2+, Na+, K+, Zn2+, Cl–, and NO3–), dyes, and molecules (aniline, naphthol, MB, and MG) was studied and there was no significant change in emission intensities other than CR (Figure 6b). With the addition of CR, the intensity of N,S-CDs was quenched after 50 s. This result confirmed the fast response time of N,S-CDs in the detection of CR (Figure S7,SI). The fluorescence response of N,S-CDs for CR was compared to the previous results (Table S2,SI). The study showed that N,S-CDs have high sensitivity, selectivity, and quick response time and can be good sensors for CR.
## Possible Mechanism
To investigate the quenching mechanism, the absorbance of CR and fluorescence excitation as well as the emission spectra of N,S-CDs were recorded. A good overlap between the absorption spectrum of CR with the excitation and emission spectra of N,S-CDs was observed (Figure 7a), which suggests that the fluorescence quenching may be due to the inner filter effect or fluorescence resonance energy transfer. To confirm the mechanism, the ζ-potentials of N,S-CDs and CR were carried out. The ζ-potentials of N,S-CDs (−10.7) and CR (−39.6) (Figure S6a,c, SI) were both negative in charge; so there must be Coulombic repulsion between them, which increases their distance. Therefore, the quenching of N,S-CDs’ emission intensity may not be due to FRET. The lifetime study of N,S-CDs in the presence and absence of CR has been done to confirm the mechanism. As shown in Figure 7b, the lifetime of N,S-CDs was 3.93 ns without CR and 4.09 ns with CR, respectively, which suggests that the fluorescence quenching of N,S-CDs was due to IFE. The quenching mechanism was also described by using the Stern–Volmer equation (eq 4)
where I0 and I are the fluorescence intensities of N,S-CDs in the absence and presence of CR, [Q] is the concentration of CR, and Ksv is the Stern–Volmer constant. The plot of I0/I versus [Q] (Figure S7, SI) exhibited a linear relationship within the concentration range for CR (0–50 μM). The bimolecular quenching constant was calculated from the equation Ksv = kqτ, where kq is the bimolecular quenching constant, and τ is the average lifetime of the N,S-CDs. The obtained value for Ksv (3.4 × 104 L mol–1) was determined from the plot, and the average lifetime was measured to be 3.93 ns, which resulted in a kq value of 8.6 × 1012 L mol–1 s–1. This calculated value was greater than the maximum value possible for dynamic quenching (2 × 1010 L mol–1 s–1). Therefore, the expected quenching interactions between the N,S-CDs and CR were static in nature.
## Colorimetric Sensing of Congo Red
The detection of CR was also performed by the colorimetric method. With the increase in the concentration of CR, the peak of N,S-CD at 277 nm gradually decreases, and the peak at 338 nm gradually increases. A wide peak was also observed from 430 to 580 nm (Figure 8a). The absorbance at 338 nm was taken as a major peak for the colorimetric detection probe. A good linear correlation was obtained in the linear range of 1–7 and 10–60 μM with R2 = 0.992 and 0.997, respectively (Figure 8c,d). The linear equations for 1–7 and 10–60 μM were y = (0.0162 ± 5.88 × 10–4) × x + (1.043 ± 0.0025) and y = (0.021 ± 4.97 × 10–4) × x + (0.968 ± 0.0193), respectively. The limit of detection was 530 nM in the linear range of 1–7 μM. There was a visible change in the color of the N,S-CDs with the addition of varying concentrations of CR. In daylight, the color was changed from light yellow to bright orange and green to red in UV light, respectively (Figure 9a,b).

## Antioxidant Activity
Antioxidant activity is a widely used parameter to measure the capability of a material to scavenge free radicals. Generally, nanosized materials provide oxidative stress in biological systems. However, some of the carbon dots showed antioxidant activity., Here, we have studied the antioxidant activity of the N,S-CDs using DPPH-based assay. DPPH is a nitrogen-containing long-lived free radical and deep purple in color. On interaction with an antioxidant, the color changes to yellow. The percentage of inhibition can be evaluated by measuring the absorbance at 517 nm compared to the blank. Figure S8 demonstrates that with the addition of 0.75 mg/mL–1 CDs, the absorbance of the resulting DPPH solution decreased at 517 nm and equilibrated at 8 min. Various concentrations of CDs were added to a 100 μM methanolic DPPH solution. The radical scavenging activity of N,S-CDs increased in a manner that depended on the dosage. As the concentration of N,S-CDs rose from 0.1 to 1 mg/mL–1, the absorbance of DPPH at 517 nm decreased, reaching a saturation at 0.75 mg/mL–1 (Figure 10a). The percentage of radical scavenging activity against the concentration of N,S-CDs is illustrated in Figure 10b. The EC50 of N,S-CDs was found to be 0.26 mg/mL–1 and the EC50 of ascorbic acid was 0.002 mg/mL–1.
## Conclusions
In conclusion, we have synthesized and fabricated fluorescent N,S-CDs from a single natural precursor, the Giloy stem, by a hydrothermal method and fabricated an efficient fluorescent/colorimetric probe for the detection of organic pollutants CR and 4-NP. In addition, the N,S-CDs exhibited an antioxidant behavior. The N,S-CDs showed good sensitivity and selectivity toward the fluorescence detection of 4-NP and CR with LODs of 380 and 62 nM, respectively. The fluorescence quenching detection involved an inner filter mechanism. More interestingly, in addition to the fluorescence technique, CR was monitored through colorimetric and smartphone techniques. Smartphone technology is highly preferred for real-time detection of analytes due to its good image recognition capability and computing power. The proposed probe showed a significant percentage of recovery for both CR and 4-NP in real water samples, which explains the practicability of N,S-CDs as multimode probes. The performance of the N,S-CDs under examination includes not only the sensitive multimode detection of organic pollutants but also efficient antioxidant activity.
