# Revealing the vertical structure of in-situ fabricated perovskite nanocrystals films toward efficient pure red light-emitting diodes
## Abstract
AbstractThe development of efficient perovskite light-emitting diodes (PeLEDs) relies strongly on the fabrication of perovskite films with rationally designed structures (grain size, composition, surface, etc.). Therefore, an understanding of structure-performance relationships is of vital importance for developing high-performance perovskite devices, particularly for devices with in-situ fabricated perovskite nanocrystal films. In this study, we reveal the vertical structure of an in-situ fabricated quasi-two-dimensional perovskite film. By combining time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry, energy dispersive spectroscopy, grazing incidence wide-angle X-ray scattering (GIWAXS), and low-temperature photoluminescence spectra, we illustrate that the resulting in-situ fabricated DPPA2Csn-1Pbn(Br0.3I0.7)3n+1 (DPPA+: 3,3-diphenylpropylammonium) film has a gradient structure with a very thin layer of ligands on the surface, predominantly small-n domains at the top, and predominantly large-n domains at the bottom owing to the solubility difference of the precursors. In addition, GIWAXS measurements show that the domain of n = 2 on the top layer has an ordered in-plane alignment. Based on the understanding of the film structure, we developed an in-situ fabrication process with ligand exchange to achieve efficient pure red PeLEDs at 638 nm with an average external quantum efficiency (EQE) of 7.4%. The optimized device had a maximum luminance of 623 cd/m2 with a peak EQE of 9.7%.Keywords: Light-emitting diodes, Perovskite, TOF-SIMS, Vertical structure, in-situ fabrication
## 1. Introduction
Perovskite light-emitting diodes (PeLEDs) are emerging as a promising display technology owing to their high color purity and ease of solution processability , , . The intrinsic ionic nature and low formation enthalpy of halide perovskites make it possible for the solution fabrication to obtain high-crystallinity films in-situ, which is a major advantage over previous light-emitting diode (LED) technologies [,,, , , , , , , ]. To date, the maximum external quantum efficiencies (EQEs) of green and near-infrared PeLEDs fabricated by in-situ fabrication methods have exceeded 20% , , , . In contrast, the development of pure red and blue PeLEDs still lags far behind [,]. One of the main challenges is balancing the spectral tunability and charge injection of in-situ fabricated perovskite nanocrystal films [,, , , , , , ]. To solve this problem, many strategies, including composition and ligand engineering, have been successfully demonstrated [,,, , , ]. Among these strategies, quasi-two-dimensional (quasi-2D) perovskites with an energy funnel effect have been proven to be effective for achieving high-performance devices [,, , , , , , ]. However, there are only a few studies that have focused on the vertical structure-performance relationships in these in-situ fabricated quasi-2D perovskite films.
In the field of perovskite solar cells, numerous studies have been devoted to investigating the microstructure of quasi-2D perovskite films, resulting in significant improvements in device efficiency and stability , , , , , . It has been demonstrated that the vertical distribution of the composition and the alignment direction of low-dimensional domains in perovskite films play an important role in the extraction of photogenerated carriers [,,]. Typically, the vertical microstructure of films can be characterized using grazing incidence wide-angle X-ray scattering (GIWAXS) , energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) mapping , and excitation intensity-dependent photoluminescence (PL) mapping . In comparison with perovskite films for solar cells, the as-fabricated perovskite films of PeLED devices are very thin, with a typical thickness of 30–50 nm, making them very difficult to characterize. On the other hand, angle dependent GIWAXS is invalid for vertical structure characterization owing to the high penetration ability of X-rays. Although EDS mapping has been applied to characterize the vertical structure of PeLEDs, the details remain unclear because of the limited spatial resolution . To the best of our knowledge, only a few studies have focused on the vertical structures of PeLEDs.
Time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (TOF-SIMS) is a highly sensitive analytical technique for investigating composition distribution by combining secondary-ion mass spectrometry and ion beam sputtering , , , . Although TOF-SIMS has the potential to provide a precise characterization of the vertical composition distribution in PeLEDs, the organic species in perovskite films inevitably decompose into fragments when interacting with high-energy sputtering ions, such as Ar+ and Cs+, thus weakening their ability to analyze the analysis ability toward ligands . To solve this problem, we investigated the vertical structure of in-situ fabricated quasi-2D all inorganic perovskite film with TOF-SIMS using ion cluster C60 as sputtering ion sources. By combing EDS mapping, GIWAXS, and low-temperature PL spectra with TOF-SIMS, we found that the as-fabricated DPPA2Csn-1Pbn(Br0.3I0.7)3n+1 (per-DPPA) film had a gradient structure with a very thin layer of ligands on the surface, small-n domains at the top, and large-n domains at the bottom of the perovskite layer. In addition, the GIWAXS results indicated that the small domain of n = 2 on the top layer had an ordered in-plane alignment. Based on the understanding of the film structure, we developed an in-situ fabrication process with ligand exchange to achieve efficient pure red PeLEDs at 638 nm with an average EQE of 7.4% (± 2.3%). The optimized device had a maximum luminance of 623.4 cd/m2 with a peak EQE of 9.7%.
## 2. Material and methods
## 2.1. Materials
All reagents were used as received without further purification: lead (II) iodide (PbI2, 99%, Aladdin), cesium iodide (CsI, 99.9%, Aladdin), lead (II) bromide (PbBr2, 99%, Aladdin), hydrobromic acid (HBr 48 wt% in water, Aladdin), 3,3-diphenylpropylamine (DPPA, 97%, Energy Chemical), n-butylamine (n-BA, 99%, Energy Chemical), 2-propanol (IPA, 99.5%, Extra Dry, with molecular sieves, water ≤ 50 ppm (by K.F.), Energy Seal), ethyl acetate (EA, 99.8%, Extra Dry, with molecular sieves, water ≤ 50 ppm (by K.F.), Energy Seal), N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF, 99.8%, Super Dry, with molecular sieves, J&K Seal), chlorobenzene (CB, 99%, J&K Seal), toluene (analytical-grade, Beijing Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China), lithium fluoride (LiF, 99.85%, Alfa Aesar), poly(ethylenedioxythiophene): poly(styrene sulfonate) (PEDOT: PSS, Heraeus-Clevios P VP AI4083), poly[(9,9-dioctylfluorenyl-2,7-diyl)-alt-(4,4′-(N-(4-butylphenyl) (TFB), 1,3,5-Tris(1-phenyl-1H-benzimidazol-2-yl)benzene (TPBi), phenylethylammonium bromide (PEABr), phenylethylammonium iodide (PEAI), and 4-phenylbuylammonium bromide (PBABr) were purchased from Xi'an Polymer Light Technology Corp.
## 2.2. Methods
## 2.2.1. Synthesis of DPPABr and n-Butylammonium bromide (n-BABr)
DPPABr was carried out via the reaction between DPPA and HBr, as previously reported . First, DPPA in absolute ethanol was stirred and cooled below 10 °C. This was followed by the addition of HBr in a 1:1 molar ratio. The reaction mixture was stirred for 2 h in an ice bath. Excess solvent was then removed using rotary evaporation. White DPPABr powder was purified three times with ethyl acetate and dried in a vacuum oven (60 °C, 12 h) for future use. n-BABr was synthesized using the same method, except that DPPA was replaced by n-BA.
## 2.2.2. Fabrication of quasi-2D per-DPPA, (n-C4H9NH3)2Csn-1Pbn(Br0.3I0.7)3n+1 (per-n-BA), (C8H9NH3)2Csn-1Pbn(Br0.3I0.7)3n+1 (per-PEA), and (C10H12NH3)2Csn-1Pbn(Br0.3I0.7)3n+1 (per-PBA) films
A mixture of DPPABr, CsI, PbBr2, and PbI2 with a molar ratio of x: 1: 0.2: 0.8 (x = 0.25, 0.50, 0.75, 1.00, or 1.25) were dissolved in DMF to obtain a 0.15 M precursor solution. The characterization and device fabrication of per-DPPA and PeLEDs correspond to x = 0.75 unless specified otherwise. The precursor was stirred for 2 h and then filtered through a 0.22 μm nylon filter before spin coating. The spin-coating rate was 4000 rpm, and toluene was dropped on the spinning substrate at 6 s from the beginning and then annealed at 95 °C for 10 min to remove the residual solvent and ensure the complete reaction of the precursors. The per-n-BA, per-PEA, and per-PBA films were fabricated using the same method described above, except for the variation in ligands. All experiments were performed in a nitrogen-filled glovebox.
## 2.2.3. Film washing and ligands exchange
After the pristine films of per-DPPA were fabricated using the above method, a mixture solvent of IPA (20% of total volume) and EA was dropped on the per-DPPA film spinning at a speed of 4000 rpm. After 40 s, the ligand exchange process was carried out by dropping PEAX (X = Br and I, Br: I = 1: 1) ligands solution on the washed per-DPPA film spinning at the same speed. The films were then annealed at 90 °C for 5 min. The ligand solution was obtained by dissolving 4.5 mg of PEABr and 5.7 mg of PEAI in 4 mL of IPA and EA mixed solvents.
## 2.2.4. Fabrication of LED Devices
Transparent ITO-coated glass substrates, with working areas of 4 mm2, were sequentially cleaned in detergent, deionized water, acetone, ethanol, and isopropanol by sonication. The cleaned substrates were treated with O2 plasma for 2 min to make the surface hydrophilic. Subsequently, PEDOT: PSS was spin-coated at 4000 rpm for 60 s and baked at 150 °C for 15 min in ambient air. Thereafter, the substrates were transferred into a nitrogen-filled glovebox and TFB (6 mg/mL in chlorobenzene) was spin-coated onto the PEDOT: PSS film at 4000 rpm for 40 s and annealed at 120 °C for 30 min. The TFB film was then treated with O2 plasma for 5 s to improve wettability. Per-DPPA films were fabricated as described above with x = 0.75, and the anti-solvent dropping time was 6 s from the beginning. For the optimized device, additional film washing and ligand exchange processes were required, as described above. TPBi, LiF, and Al layers were thermally evaporated with thicknesses of 50, 1, and 100 nm, respectively, in a high-vacuum thermal evaporator at a pressure below 2.0 × 10−4 Pa.
## 2.2.5. Optical characterizations
Ultraviolet visible (UV-vis) absorption spectra of the per-DPPA films were measured using a UV-6100 UV-vis spectrophotometer (Shanghai Mapada Instruments Co., Ltd.), and PL spectra were obtained using a F-380 fluorescence spectrometer (Tianjin Gangdong Science and Technology Development Co., Ltd.). Low-temperature-dependent PL spectra measurements were performed using a HORIBA Scientific FL3-2IHR with a temperature range of 80–300 K.
## 2.2.6. Scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM), EDS mapping, atomic force microscopy (AFM), transmission electron microscopy (TEM), and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) characterization
The cross-sectional sample for STEM observations was prepared (carried out in Toray Research Center) by first depositing a thin layer of Pt (3 nm thick), followed by etching with a focused ion beam utilizing FEI 1td. Bright-field (BF) STEM and high-angle annular dark field (HAADF) STEM images were captured on a JEOL-JEM-ARM 200F Dual-X machine operating at an acceleration voltage of 200 kV. EDS mapping was performed using a J-EOL JED2300T instrument. AFM images were obtained using a Bruker Dimension Fastscan AFM in the non-contact mode. TEM images were acquired using a FEI Talos F200S field-emission TEM (FEI Co., USA) operated at 200 kV. The SEM samples were deposited on cleaned single-polished silicon substrates and measured using an S8230 (Hitachi, Ltd.).
## 2.2.7. GIWAXS and X-ray diffraction (XRD) characterization
The GIWAXS measurements were performed at beamline BL17B1 of the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility60. The wavelength of the X-ray beam was 1.24 Å. The scattering patterns were obtained with a grazing incidence angle of 0.1° and an exposure time of 5 s. The distance between the sample and detector was 240 mm. The XRD patterns were recorded using a BRUKER D8 Advance X-ray diffractometer equipped with a Cu Kα radiation source (wavelength of 1.5405 Å).
## 2.2.8. Devices characterization
Typical electroluminescence (EL) spectra were obtained using a dual-channel fiber-optic spectrometer (AvaSpec-ULS2048-2-USB2). The current density-voltage and luminance-voltage characteristics of the PeLED devices were measured using a connected Keithley 2400 power source analyzer and spectroradiometer (Photo Research Inc., PR-655). The EQE of the device was calculated according to the Lambertian profile.
## 2.2.9. TOF-SIMS characterization
TOF-SIMS samples were prepared by spin-coating the precursor solution onto an O2-plasma treated Si wafer for 2 min. The measurements were conducted with a PHI nano ToF II (ULVAC-PHI Inc., Japan) using C60 as the sputtering ion source and a 2 s period for every sputtering cycle. The area of the analysis was 50 μm × 50 μm and the sputtering area was 400 μm × 400 μm.
## 3. Results and discussion
The quasi-2D film of per-DPPA was fabricated by adapting our previously reported on-chip in-situ ligand-assisted reprecipitation (LARP) process . A solution of DPPABr, CsI, PbBr2, and PbI2 with a molar ratio of x: 1: 0.2: 0.8 in DMF was used as the spin-coating precursor solution. Importantly, the amount of DPPABr in the precursor solutions was varied to obtain pure red emission. Fig. 1a shows the PL and absorption spectra of the per-DPPA films. The PL peaks blue-shifted from 669 to 575 nm as the molar ratio between DPPABr and Pb increased from 0.25 to 1.25. Meanwhile, the excitonic peaks corresponding to small-n domains were distinguished in the absorption spectra with an increasing molar ratio of DPPABr. As shown in Fig. 1b, the per-DPPA film obtained from a precursor solution, with a DPPABr: Pb molar ratio of 0.75, showed pure red emission at 626.6 nm, as visualized under UV light illumination. This film had a uniform thickness of approximately 70 nm, as shown in Fig. 1c.
Spin-coating of the perovskite solution is known to be accompanied by solvent evaporation and solute crystallization . The spin-coated quasi-2D perovskite film may have a composition or domain distribution owing to the solubility difference of the perovskite precursors in the DMF solution. In a typical crystallization process, a substance with low solubility first precipitates at the interface of the solid and liquid. This results in a film with a gradient composition distribution in which the substances with low solubility concentrate at the region near the substrate and substances with large solubility concentrate at the top layer of the film. By combining TOF-SIMS, chemical etching methods, and EDS mapping measurements, we demonstrated that the obtained film had a gradient composition distribution in the vertical direction with ligands dominating the surface, iodine concentrated near the top part of the film, and bromine concentrated near the region close to the substrate as schematically represented in Fig. 2a. It is known that CsPbBr3 is almost insoluble in DMF, and CsPbI3 has a solubility of ∼ 0.6 mmol/mL, while the ligand has a much larger solubility in DMF. The distribution order of these substances in the film correlates with the solubility order in DMF, which confirms that the crystallization of our film is a solubility-controlled process. Because of the higher ligand content near the surface, the quasi-2D film also had a gradient distribution of domains with small-n domains dominating near the surface region of the film, and large-n domains dominating near the regions close to the substrate.
In a typical TOF-SIMS depth profiling characterization, sputter ion sources and probe ion sources alternatively impact the surface . Subsequently, the ejected ions or ion fragments sputtered by the probe ion source were collected and analyzed using mass spectrometry. Fig. 2c, d shows the results of the vertical composition distribution obtained by applying TOF-SIMS measurements. Fig. 2b shows the typical depth profiling curves of positive ions measured using TOF-SIMS along the vertical film direction. The content of the ligand DPPA+ significantly decreased around the surface layer and then continuously decreased with film thinning. In contrast, the Pb2+ ions showed a moderate decrease and Cs+ showed a slight decrease with film thinning. These results suggest that the ligands of DPPA+ were dominant on the upper surface of the film, while most ions of Cs+ were located in the middle and bottom parts of the film. The concentration of Pb2+ was much higher on the surface and decreased slightly with film thinning. As shown in Fig. 2c, the depth profiling curves of the I− and Br− ions are different, with a possible complementary relationship. The I− ions were concentrated at the top part of the film, while Br− ions were concentrated in the regions close to the substrate. Fig. 2d shows the depth profile of the negative-ion fragments. The distribution of [PbBrxI3-x]− fragments emerged in the sequence [PbI3]−, [PbBr1I2]−, [PbBr2I1]−, and [PbBr3]−. These results support the conclusion that the obtained per-DPPA film had a gradient structure.
To confirm the universality of this solubility-derived composition distribution, we further analyzed the vertical structure of other quasi-2D perovskite systems by applying TOF-SIMS depth profiling, including per-n-BA, per-PEA, and per-PBA. As shown in Fig. S1, the positive ion depth-profiling curves (Fig. S1a, Fig. S1b, and Fig. S1c) show that the signals of the organic ligands decreased significantly around the film surface, while the Pb2+ ions showed a moderate decrease and Cs+ showed a gentle decrease along the vertical direction. These are consistent with the tendency of per-DPPA. The observed similar trend of element distributions along the vertical direction suggests that the formation of the gradient structure was universal for the in-situ fabricated quasi-2D film owing to the solubility difference. It is noticed that the complementary relationships between Br− and I− are absent for the per-n-BA, per-PEA, and per-PBA perovskite films, which is possible because the coordination ability of these three kinds of ligands to perovskite is smaller than that of DPPABr .
The obtained per-DPPA film was further characterized using STEM observations with EDS mapping of the cross-sectional sample. As shown in Figs. 2e and S2a, b, STEM measurements indicate that the films are composed of small-sized nanoparticles with diameters of approximately 5–10 nm. Fig. 2f shows the elemental distributions of I, C, Cs, Pb, and Br of a typical per-DPPA film on the PEDOT layer. The C signal intensity decreased significantly near the surface and then decreased gradually inside the film. The Cs signal intensity in the region near the substrate was stronger than that in the region near the surface. The Br signal intensity curve is similar to that of Cs, whereas the tendency of the I signal intensity is opposite to that of Cs. The intensity of the Pb signal showed a slight change inside the film, except for a significant decrease near the surface. The change trend of these elements is consistent with the results obtained from the TOF-SIMS analysis, thus providing additional evidence of the gradient vertical structure.
To confirm the formation of small-n domains dominated near the surface, the obtained per-DPPA film was washed with a mixed solvent of EA and IPA (20% of total volume) and then characterized using low-temperature PL spectra and GIWAXS measurements. Fig. 3a shows the PL spectra before and after washing at 80 K. The full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the PL emission decreased after washing (Fig. S3). The emission peaks at 566 nm and 591 nm correspond to n domain values of n = 2 and n = 3, respectively, which weakened after washing. Based on integrated GIWAXS characterization (Figs. 3b and S4), the diffraction peak at q = 0.717 Å−1, corresponding to a domain value of n = 2 (060), completely disappeared after washing. Contrarily, the intensity of the diffraction peak at q = 0.822 Å−1 (n = 3, (080)) remained almost unchanged after washing. Based on these analyses, the small-n domain of n = 2 was completely removed after washing, whereas the small-n domain of n = 3 was only partially removed. Moreover, we studied the alignment orientation of the low-dimensional domains on the top layer in detailed analysis of the GIWAXS results. As shown in Fig. 3c, the diffraction pattern corresponding to n = 2 (060) is present as a spot along the qz direction, thereby proving its in-plane alignment, which is diminished after washing, as shown in Fig. 3d. The orders of the other domains in the film dropped significantly compared to the n = 2 domain, which is proven by the appearance of ring-like diffraction patterns.
Based on the element distribution analysis and dimension characterizations above, the obtained per-DPPA film had a gradient vertical structure with an in-plane aligned domain of n = 2 dominated at the top layer. Based on a previous report by Zhang et al., such a structure is not beneficial for carrier injection from the top of the film because of the thick organic layer and the anisotropy of conductivity in low-dimensional perovskites . To achieve better carrier injection and device performance, we designed an in-situ ligand-exchange process to fabricate high-quality perovskite films for efficient devices. As shown in Fig. 4a, the pristine per-DPPA film was prepared by adapting the in-situ LARP method, followed by a washing process with a mixed solvent of IPA and EA and a ligand exchange process using a solution of PEABr and PEAI dissolved in a mixed solvent of IPA and EA. The AFM measurements (Fig. S5) show that the film thickness changed from ∼ 70 to ∼ 50 nm and then to ∼ 40 nm with washing and ligand exchange. The evolution of these films was monitored using PL and absorption measurements. As shown in Fig. 4b, the PL intensity dropped to 10% of its original value after film washing and recovered to 43 % of its original value after ligand exchange. In addition, the absorption peak at approximately 560 nm (n = 2) disappeared, corresponding to the removal of the small-n in-plane dimensional structure (Fig. 4c).
Based on the optimization of the film energy band structure by the aforementioned post-treatment process, we constructed the following device structure (Fig. 5a): ITO/PEDOT: PSS/TFB/per-DPPA/TPBi/LiF/Al. Because of the gradient distribution of bromine and iodine in the film, the energy band positions at various positions in the film are slightly different. The band gap is wider at the bottom layer because bromide dominates in this region, and the valence band maximum gradually rises owing to the modulation of ligands on the effective work function . Fig. 5b compares the device's current density and brightness before and after the post-treatment process. It can be seen that the current density increased by approximately two orders of magnitude after washing and ligand exchange, which shows that the electron injection barrier on the upper surface was greatly reduced after the post-treatment. Fig. S6 shows the electroluminescence (EL) spectra of the optimized device at 3, 4, and 5 V. The spectrum had a central wavelength of 638 nm, a FWHM of 37 nm, and a red shift at higher voltages. Due to the significant increasing in the electron injection, the maximum brightness increased from 3.5 cd/m2 to 623 cd/m2, thus resulting in an increase of nearly three orders of magnitude. In addition, the corresponding device efficiency also increased from 2% to 9.7% (Fig. 5c), which is among the highest results of pure red PeLEDs in the display band (Fig. S7 and Table S1). As shown in Fig. 5d, the average EQE was 7.5% after counting 40 devices, and this proves that the devices prepared using this process have good repeatability. We also tested device stability with an initial brightness of 100 cd/m2 at a constant voltage of 4.0 V. As shown in Fig. S8, the lifetime of T50 (the time when the brightness drops to 50% of its initial value) was approximately 6 min. The EL spectrum of the device exhibited an evident red shift under high-voltage operation. This phenomenon can be explained by the phase separation of the mixed halogen perovskite under an external bias.
## 4. Conclusion
Combined with TOF-SIMS depth profiling, EDS line mapping, GIWAXS, and chemical etching methods, we found that owing to the solubility difference of each component in the solution, the in-situ fabricated quasi-2D perovskite film showed a gradient structure distribution in the vertical direction with a thin layer of ligands on the surface, predominantly small-n domains at the top, and predominantly large-n domains at the bottom. Moreover, small-n domains were iodine-concentrated with an in-plane alignment domain of n = 2 on the top layer. The large-n domains were bromine concentrated near the region close to the substrate. Based on the understanding of the vertical structure in quasi-2D perovskite films, we developed an in-situ fabrication process with ligand exchange to modify the film energy band structure, thereby making the injection of electrons more efficient. Finally, we obtained a pure red efficient PeLEDs with an emissive wavelength of 638 nm and a maximum EQE of 9.7%. Our results provide a comprehensive understanding of the vertical composition distribution of quasi-2D perovskite films and aims to serve as a reference for researchers to accurately control the film microstructure for high-performance PeLEDs.
