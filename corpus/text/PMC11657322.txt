# Synthesis and characterization of hydroxyapatite–zinc oxide nanocomposites incorporating rosemary and thyme essential oils for enhanced bone regeneration and antimicrobial activity
## Abstract
The study presents a novel approach to bone regeneration using bioactive nanocomposites. The research focuses on creating hydroxyapatite (HAp) and zinc oxide (ZnO) nanocomposites functionalized with rosemary and thyme essential oils (EOs) to enhance osteogenic and antimicrobial properties. The findings reveal that the incorporation of EOs not only improves biocompatibility by reducing Zn-induced cytotoxicity but also enhances antimicrobial activity, particularly against Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria. Additionally, thyme EO exhibits a more potent effect in enhancing cell viability and antibacterial efficacy compared to rosemary EO. Structural analyses through X-ray diffraction (XRD), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and Fourier-transform infrared (FT–IR) spectroscopy confirm the successful synthesis of well-integrated HAp-ZnO structures with the EOs. This study offers promising insights into the potential of functionalized nanocomposites for biomedical applications, especially in bone regeneration and infection prevention.
## Introduction
Osteoporosis (OP) and low bone mass, which are manifestations of low bone mineral density (LBMD), represent chronic metabolic bone diseases characterized by reduced bone mass and compromised microstructure. These conditions significantly increase the risk of fractures in various regions of the body, thereby posing a substantial public health challenge. The global impact of OP has placed considerable strain on advancements in social, economic, and medical fields. In the United States alone, OP currently affects over 10 million individuals, with projections suggesting that by 2020, approximately 14 million people over the age of 50 will be impacted. Globally, about 200 million women are affected by OP, underscoring the widespread nature of this condition []. Bone regeneration is an extraordinary physiological process that allows for the complete restoration of damaged bone tissue without scarring. However, in cases involving large segmental defects caused by trauma or cancer, the bone repair process often fails, leading to delayed healing or nonunion. Addressing these challenges requires a profound understanding of bone regeneration mechanisms, as current therapeutic approaches remain insufficient for effectively treating such conditions []. Calcium phosphate-based materials, particularly hydroxyapatite (HAp), have garnered significant attention due to their osteoconductive and, in some instances, osteoinductive properties, which are pivotal for bone regeneration. The release of calcium and phosphorus ions from these materials have a key role in regulating the activity of osteoblasts and osteoclasts, thereby promoting bone regeneration. Furthermore, the surface characteristics and porosity of calcium phosphate materials influence cell adhesion, protein interactions, and bone mineral synthesis, making them highly desirable for use in bone regeneration applications []. HAp, a naturally occurring form of calcium phosphate, is the main mineral component of teeth and bones. Its physical and chemical resemblance to natural bone, along with its biocompatibility, biodegradability, and bioactivity, makes HAp an invaluable material in various interdisciplinary scientific fields, including biology, chemistry, and medicine. The compositional similarity of calcium phosphate-based ceramics to bone mineral enhances their bioactivity and osteoconductivity, making them attractive alternatives to synthetic bone grafts []. One of the most important elements in the human body is zinc (Zn), which plays a vital role in numerous physiological processes, particularly in bone metabolism. Zn-containing materials have been shown to enhance bone regeneration by promoting angiogenesis, osteogenic activity, and cell proliferation while simultaneously inhibiting osteoclast development []. In bone tissue, Zn is predominantly found in bone apatite and is a key component of various enzymes []. Zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO NPs) have emerged as promising alternatives to traditional antibiotics, demonstrating remarkable efficacy against a wide range of drug-resistant microbes. Additionally, ZnO NPs have been shown to accelerate bone formation and mineralization, further underscoring their potential in bone regeneration []. Rosemary essential oil (EO) is another natural product with potent antibacterial and antioxidant properties. Rosemary EO is known for its antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, anti-tumorigenic, antioxidant, antinociceptive, anti-apoptotic, and neuroprotective effects. Moreover, it has been shown to have therapeutic benefits on mood, memory, learning, pain, anxiety, and sleep [, ]. Thymus, a genus within the Lamiaceae family, is an herbaceous perennial or subshrub that is widely distributed across the globe. Thymus EOs, particularly thymol (2-isopropyl-5-methylphenol), the primary component of thyme EO, have attracted significant interest due to their diverse biological activities. Traditional medicine has long utilized thymol and thyme EO as expectorant, antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, antiviral, and antiseptic agents, particularly for therapies against upper respiratory disorders [, ].
## Aim
The present study aims to synthesize a composite material based on HAp and ZnO, incorporating rosemary and thyme EOs. This composite is designed to enhance bone regeneration while also providing antimicrobial effects, leveraging the bioactive properties of the EOs.
## Materials and Methods
## Reagents and chemicals
The experimental procedures were carried out using the following chemicals: diammonium phosphate [(NH4)2HPO4, 98%] and calcium nitrate tetrahydrate [Ca(NO3)2•4H2O, 99%], both procured from Sigma–Aldrich. Ammonium hydroxide (NH4OH, 25% solution) was employed as a pH adjuster, while zinc acetate dihydrate (C4H6O4Zn•2H2O) was obtained from Fluka–Honeywell Research Chemicals. Additionally, distilled water served as the solvent throughout the synthesis processes. For the functionalization of the synthesized materials, rosemary and thyme EOs were utilized as natural bioactive agents.
## Material synthesis
HAp was synthesized using the synthWAVE apparatus (Milestone Srl, Sorisole, Bergamo, Italy) through a process of co-precipitation then the obtained precipitate was treated by using hydrothermal maturation in a microwave field. The synthWAVE apparatus operates by filling vials with the necessary reagents, which are then placed on a rotating platform and securely sealed with Teflon covers. During the process, a mechanical stirrer is automatically lowered to ensure thorough mixing. The vials are submerged in a liquid medium, typically water, which effectively absorbs microwave energy, facilitating both heat transfer and energy input to the samples. To prevent the solvents from boiling, an automated clamp and nitrogen pressure system are employed to seal the sample chamber. Upon completion of the process, an integrated cooling system rapidly reduces the temperature within the chamber. This method offers several advantages, including simplicity, reduced synthesis time, reproducibility of conditions established in small-scale reactions, and ease of parameter adjustments.
The synthesis of HAp was initiated by dissolving calcium nitrate tetrahydrate and diammonium phosphate powders in distilled water, maintaining a calcium-to-phosphate (Ca/P) ratio of 1.67, which is characteristic of HAp. The phosphate precursor was added dropwise into the calcium nitrate solution under regular magnetic stirring. After thorough homogenization, the pH of the solution was modified to approximately 10 by gradually adding a 25 wt.% ammonium hydroxide solution. The prepared mixture was then transferred to the microwave-assisted apparatus for hydrothermal maturation, which was conducted at 250°C under the first pressure of two bars for 30 minutes.
Upon completion of the hydrothermal treatment, the resulting solution was washed with distilled water and filtered through filter paper to remove excess ammonia, subsequently adjusting the pH to around 7. The final HAp powder was acquired by drying the filtered biomaterial in an oven at 37°C for 24 hours.
ZnO was synthesized utilizing a precipitation method, then the obtained solution was treated by hydrothermal treatment in the microwave field. Zinc acetate dihydrate was dissolved in distilled water with continuous magnetic stirring, and the pH of the reaction mixture was adjusted to around 10 using a 25 wt.% ammonium hydroxide solution. The resulting precipitate was then subjected to hydrothermal treatment in the microwave-assisted apparatus at 160°C under an initial pressure of five bars for 20 minutes. After hydrothermal processing, the solution was washed with distilled water, filtered, and the pH adjusted to approximately 7. The final ZnO powder was dried in the oven at 37°C for 24 hours.
The dried HAp and ZnO powders were then mixed in a 1:1 ratio using a mortar. The resulting nanocomposite was further functionalized by incorporating rosemary and thyme EOs, using chloroform as a solvent. The EOs were added in two different concentrations (2.5% and 5%, v/v), resulting in the following samples: HAp-ZnO-EO1C1 and HAp-ZnO-EO1C2 for the rosemary EO, and HAp-ZnO-EO2C1 and HAp-ZnO-EO2C2 for the thyme EO.
## Morphological and structural characterization
## X-ray diffraction (XRD)
The crystalline phases present in the synthesized powders were identified using XRD analysis. For this purpose, a PANalytical Empyrean diffractometer (Malvern PANalytical, Bruno, The Netherlands) was employed. The instrument was equipped with a hybrid monochromator (2xGe 220) on the incident side, which allowed for precise control of the X-ray beam. On the diffracted side, a parallel plate collimator was mounted on a PIXcel three-dimensional (3D) detector to ensure accurate detection of the diffracted X-rays.
XRD measurements were conducted at room temperature (RT). The analysis was performed with an incidence angle of 0.5° and covered Bragg angle (2θ) values ranging from 10° to 80°. The acquisition period for each measurement was set to 255 seconds, with a step size of 0.01414°, providing high-resolution data. The radiation source used was Cu Kα, with a wavelength of 1.5406 Å, operating at 40 mA and 45 kV. This setup allowed for the detailed characterization of the crystalline phases present in the generated powders.
## Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)
SEM analysis was conducted using a QUANTA INSPECT F50 microscope (Thermo Fisher, Eindhoven, The Netherlands). This advanced instrument is equipped with an energy-dispersive X-ray spectrometer (EDS), providing elemental analysis with a resolution of 133 eV at the Mn Kα line. Additionally, the microscope features a field-emission electron gun (FEG) that offers a high resolution of 1.2 nm, allowing for detailed imaging at the nanoscale.
To prepare the samples for SEM analysis, the powdery materials were mounted onto slide supports using carbon tape, which ensured stability and minimized movement during imaging. The prepared slides were then put into the microscope’s analysis chamber. The SEM images were collected by detecting the secondary electrons emitted from the sample’s surface, providing detailed information about the shape and size of the particles.
## Fourier-transform infrared (FT–IR) spectroscopy
By using a Thermo Nicolet 6700 FT-IR spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), equipped with a ZnSe crystal, the distinctive functional groups of the synthesized NPs were analyzed. A small powder sample was evaluated to identify the specific functional groups present. The analysis was conducted at RT, employing 32 scans of the sample to ensure accuracy and reproducibility.
The spectral data were recorded with a resolution of 4 cm–1, covering the wavenumber range from 4000 to 1000 cm–1. The FT–IR spectrometer was connected to a database and processing system, operated using the Omnic software (Thermo Nicolet, version 8.2), which facilitated the precise capture and analysis of the infrared spectra. This setup allowed for the detailed investigation of the conventional functional groups of the obtained NPs.
## In vitro interactions with osteoblast cells
By using Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM), which was boosted with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), under normal conditions of temperature and humidity (37°C, 5% CO2, and 90% humidity) MG-63 osteoblast cells (CLS, Heidelberg, Germany) were cultivated in. Prior to experimentation, the NPs were sterilized by using an ultraviolet (UV) lamp for 3–4 hours. Following sterilization, the NPs were suspended in a complete culture medium having a concentration of 2 mg/mL using ultrasonic dispersion to ensure uniform distribution.
Using a density of 5000 cells per well, the MG-63 cells were seeded into 96-well plates and incubated under basic conditions to permit the cell attachment. The initial culture medium was discharged after 24 hours and replaced with fresh culture medium containing NPs at different concentrations (0, 25, 50, 100, and 200 μg/mL). By using an ultrasound bath, the previously NP suspensions were prepared by dispersing them in the culture medium. After this process using standard conditions (temperature and humidity), the cells were then incubated with the NPs for two days, after which cell viability was analyzed.
Cell viability was calculated using the 3-(4,5-dimethyl-thiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay. After the incubation period, the NP-containing medium was removed and changed with an MTT solution in complete culture medium, followed by an additional 2-hour incubation under standard conditions. By dissolving 10% MTT, 5 mg/mL in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), Eagle’s minimum essential medium (EMEM), the MTT solution was made. The assay relies on the reduction of MTT to formazan by metabolically active cells, a process directly proportional to cell viability.
Following incubation, the MTT medium was taken out, and the formazan crystals that had formed within the cells were solubilized using dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). The concentration of formazan, which correlates with cell viability, was quantified by measuring the absorbance at a wavelength of 570 nm using a spectrophotometer.
All quantitative measurements included blank samples (NPs without cells) at the established concentrations, and the absorbance values of these blanks were subtracted from those of the cell samples. The experiments were done in triplicate, and the cell viability and differentiation were calculated relative to the negative control samples (which were assigned a value of 100%). The results were presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), and statistical significance was determined using the Student’s t-test, with p-values denoted as *p<0.05, **p<0.01, and ***p<0.001.
## Microbiological evaluation
The Microbiology Laboratory at the Faculty of Biology, University of Bucharest, Romania, provided American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) strains utilized in this research, with the purpose of testing the antimicrobial activity of the samples. The bacterial strains included Staphylococcus aureus (Gram-positive), Escherichia coli, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (both Gram-negative). Additionally, the study involved Candida albicans yeast strain.
## Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) assay
MIC was determined using a quantitative method that requires serial binary microdilutions in a liquid medium (simple broth), which were evenly added across 96-well plates. Initially, the first well of each row was treated with a concentration of 1000 μg/mL of the bioactive compound or nanosystem. Subsequently, eight binary dilutions were done using a micropipette, starting from well A, with a concentration of 1000 μg/mL, and ending with well H, which had a final concentration of 7.8125 μg/mL. Wells 11 and 12 were designated as controls: well 11 served as the bacteria-containing positive control, and well 12 as the bacteria-free negative control.
Following the dilution process, 15 μL of a microbial culture, adjusted to a 0.5 McFarland standard density, was put to each well. The plates were then incubated at 37°C for 24 hours. After the incubation period, the MIC for each drug or nanosystem was determined macroscopically by identifying the lowest concentration at which turbidity or visible microbial growth in the surrounding medium was not observed anymore [].
## Development of monospecific biofilms
To evaluate the effect of the synthesized biomaterials on biofilm formation, a quantitative method involving binary serial microdilutions in a sterile liquid medium (simple broth) was employed using 96-well plates. The initial well of each row was treated by using concentration of 1000 μg/mL of the bioactive compound or nanosystem. Following this, 12 binary dilutions were done with a micropipette, starting from well A (initial concentration: 1000 μg/mL) and finishing with well H (last concentration: 7.8125 μg/mL). After the dilutions were prepared, 15 μL of a microbial culture, standardized to a 0.5 McFarland density, was incorporated in each well. The plates were then incubated at 37°C for 24 hours to allow for biofilm formation.
Post-incubation, the biofilms were gently cleaned three times with sterile physiological water (AFS) to remove non-adherent cells and debris. The biofilms were then fixed with cold methanol for five minutes. After methanol fixation, the plates were allowed to dry, and the biofilms were stained using a 1% crystal violet solution for 20 minutes. The surplus dye was subsequently discharged by rinsing the plates with tap water. The residual dye, which stained the biofilm cells stuck to the well walls, was then solubilized using a 33% acetic acid solution.
The resulting suspensions were analyzed spectrophotometrically by measuring the absorbance at 490 nm, providing quantitative data on the extent of biofilm formation. This method allowed for the assessment of the biofilm inhibitory effects of the biomaterials.
## Results
## XRD analysis
Figure 1 presents the X-ray diffractograms for the HAp-ZnO powder synthesized via microwave-assisted hydrothermal treatment. XRD was utilized to analyze the crystallinity of the obtained sample. The XRD pattern shown in Figure 1 reveals the presence of a well-defined crystalline material, as evidenced by the narrow and sharp diffraction maxima. These diffraction peaks correspond to specific Miller indices, including (002), (121), (030), (002), (101), (130), (222), (102), (123), (004), (110), (103), and (112), which are characteristic of the HAp and ZnO phases.
The small full width at half maximum (FWHM) of these diffraction peaks indicates the formation of relatively large crystallites, with an average size of approximately 47.9 nm. When comparing these results with data from the literature and the American Society for Testing and Materials (ASTM) standards, it is confirmed that both HAp and ZnO crystallized in the hexagonal system. The sample demonstrates a high degree of crystallinity, as reflected by the significant intensity values of the diffraction maxima, which are recorded at 4850 at 31° and at 4820 at 36°.

The ZnO is characterized by a “flower-like” morphology, as seen in Figure 2F, 2G, while the HAp exhibits a rod-shaped geometry, depicted in Figure 2E. The ZnO aggregates have a size range of 2.5–6 μm, whereas the HAp nanocrystals vary in size from 10–45 nm, as shown in Figure 4B.
Figure 2A, 2B, 2C, 2D, 2E, 2F, 2G, 2H provides the SEM micrographs of the HAp/ZnO sample at various magnifications (×5000, ×10 000, ×20 000, and ×40 000). At ×5000 magnification (Figure 2A), the ZnO particles are visible as petal-shaped structures forming nanoflowers. These structures are non-homogeneously distributed and appear as agglomerates of varying sizes. In Figure 2C, 2D, HAp NPs are seen adhering to the surface of the ZnO particles, arranged in rod-like formations that aggregate on the petal surfaces. The SEM analysis confirms a good intercalation of the HAp and ZnO components, with the HAp effectively coating the surface of the ZnO nanoflowers.
SEM analysis indicates a successful intercalation of the two components – HAp and ZnO – within the sample. The HAp fibers effectively coat the surface of the ZnO nanoflowers, suggesting strong integration between the two phases.
Further observation of the ZnO nanoflowers at higher magnifications (Figure 2E, 2F, 2G, 2H) reveals the presence of additional leaf-like structures contributing to the formation of the flower-shaped ZnO nanostructures. The formation mechanism of these structures appears to involve the combination of 8–10 leaf-like petals to create the observed nanoflowers.
The energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) spectroscopy, as presented in Figure 3, confirms the presence of all the expected elements – Zn, Ca, P, and O – specific to the HAp and ZnO components. The EDX images also highlight the distribution of these elements, revealing compositionally homogeneous regions as well as areas with higher concentrations of ZnO, as depicted in Figure 5A, 5B, 5C, 5D, 5E, 5F.
Figure 4A, 4B presents histograms illustrating the size distribution of ZnO and HAp particles. The histogram for ZnO (Figure 4A) shows that the diameters of the ZnO particles range between 2.5 and 6 μm, based on SEM analysis. The most frequent particle sizes fall within the 3 to 4.5 μm and 4.5 to 6 μm ranges, indicating these particles have the largest diameters. Conversely, particles with diameters between 2.5 and 3 μm and 3.5 and 4.5 μm have the lowest frequency. Figure 4B displays the histogram for HAp, revealing that the NP sizes (diameter of the rods) range from 10 to 45 nm. The majority of these particles are between 20 and 25 nm in size. An analysis of the HAp-ZnO powder further indicates a uniform distribution of elements, with HAp effectively covering the ZnO NPs, as depicted in Figure 5A, 5B, 5C, 5D, 5E, 5F.
## FT–IR analysis
The main functional groups found in the samples were exposed using FT–IR spectroscopy, and the corresponding spectra were obtained for the samples. Figure 6A illustrates the FT–IR spectrum for the control sample (HAp-ZnO) and the samples containing rosemary EO, denoted as EO1, at two concentrations: EO1C1 (2.5%) and EO1C2 (5%).
For the control sample, several wavenumbers can be identified, with the most intense bands in the spectrum attributed to the P–O bonds within the phosphate (PO43–) group of HAp. These bands appear in the wavenumber range of 1000–1100 cm–1, specifically at 1088, 1021, and 960 cm–1. The sharp and narrow shape of the bands within this range indicates the presence of predominantly crystalline HAp. The P–O bond is also observed at 600 cm–1, while the O–H bond appears at 632 cm–1. The band attributed to the Zn–O bond begins to form at approximately 400 cm–1.
As the amount of EO in the samples increases, new, more defined bands emerge, confirming the presence of the EO. In particular, EO1C2 sample exhibits the most clearly defined bands, with the presence of O–H bonds at 3568 cm–1 and C–H bonds at 2975, 2912, 1454, and 1383 cm–1.
In Figure 6B, the FT–IR spectrum is shown for the HAp-ZnO control sample, along with the spectra for the samples containing thyme EO, denoted as EO2, which were prepared at two concentrations: EO2C1 (2.5%) and EO2C2 (5%).
Similar to the previous analysis with rosemary EO, the FT–IR results indicate a predominant presence of crystalline HAp in the samples, as evidenced by the high and narrow peaks. The most intense peaks, occurring at 1088, 1018, and 962 cm–1, are attributed to the P–O bonds characteristic of the PO43– group in HAp. Additionally, the P–O bond is observed at 600 cm–1, and the O–H bond is present at 632 cm–1. The Zn–O bond associated with ZnO is partially visible around 400 cm–1.
Regarding the bonds specific to the thyme EO, the FT–IR spectrum reveals C–C bonds from the aromatic groups within the EO, detected at 811, 1420, and 1458 cm–1. The spectrum also shows C–H bonds at 2870 and 2960 cm–1, along with an O–H bond attributed to the hydroxyl group of the EO, located at 3570 cm–1. These results have proved the incorporation of thyme EO into the HAp-ZnO matrix.
## Cell viability
Figures 7 and 8 present the results of the MTT assay conducted on HAp/ZnO/EO samples. The control HAp/ZnO samples exhibited a cytotoxic effect on osteoblast cells across all tested concentrations, with the cytotoxicity increasing proportionally to the NP concentration (**p<0.01 in comparison with negative control). However, the addition of EO1 (rosemary EO) to the NP composition resulted in a significant improvement in cell viability compared to the control. Specifically, the EO1C1 variant proved a statistically enhancement in cell viability (*p<0.05) at all investigated concentrations. Additionally, when EO1 was incorporated into the composition (in both C1 and C2 variants), the HAp-ZnO samples demonstrated a biocompatible effect at concentrations of 25 μg/mL (*p<0.05) and 50 μg/mL (*p<0.05).
Similarly, the inclusion of EO2 (thyme EO) in the HAp/ZnO NPs led to a statistically significant improvement in cell viability compared to the control NPs (**p<0.01 for concentrations between 25–100 μg/mL). In particular, the EO2C1 variant exhibited a biocompatible effect at a concentration of 25 μg/mL (**p<0.01 compared to the negative control). The EO2C2 variant demonstrated an even greater increase in viability across concentrations of 25–100 μg/mL (*p<0.05 compared to the negative control), though a slight cytotoxic effect was highlighted at the higher concentration of 200 μg/mL (**p<0.01 in comparison with negative control).
The presence of Zn in the NPs contributed to a slight cytotoxic effect on cells, which was directly proportional to the NP concentrations. To mitigate this, the introduction of EOs, particularly in the EO2C2 variant, proved to be an effective strategy for enhancing biocompatibility.
A comparative analysis of the experimental data from Figure 7 indicates that rosemary oil (EO1) effectively reduces the cytotoxicity of ZnO in HAp/ZnO NPs across all concentrations. Cell viability remains notably high at concentrations of 25 and 50 μg/mL, with the 2.5% rosemary oil concentration providing the optimal effect. Regarding thyme oil (EO2), the results shown in Figure 8 suggest that cell viability is maintained at higher levels in the presence of thyme oil, especially at lower NP concentrations, with the maximum effect observed at 25 μg/mL. Increasing the thyme oil concentration from 2.5% to 5% further supports the maintenance of cell viability, even at higher concentrations of HAp/ZnO NPs.
## Microbiological evaluation
## MIC assay
The MIC was assessed after 24 hours. The MIC values for the bacterial strains varied from 1000 μg/mL to 62.5 μg/mL, while for the yeast strains, the values were between 500 μg/mL and 250 μg/mL (Figure 9).
## Development of monospecific biofilms
The development of monospecific biofilms was analyzed after 24 hours using optical microscopy (Figure 10). The observations revealed that all samples exhibited antimicrobial effects, with those containing thyme oil demonstrating the most significant impact (Figure 11A, 11B, 11C, 11D). Across all tested concentrations, there was a substantial inhibition of bacterial strains, whereas the inhibition effect on C. albicans was minimal. These findings were further corroborated by optical microscopy, which confirmed the significant antimicrobial activity against the bacterial strains.
## Discussions
The aim of this research was to obtain an improved nanocomposite based on HAp and ZnO having thyme and rosemary EO in its composition for their antimicrobial effect. In the present literature, there is not much information based on the use of EOs in bone regeneration [].
HAp is recognized as a ceramic bone cement that closely resembles the crystalline phase of natural bone []. It offers significant advantages in biomedical applications due to its bioactivity, excellent bone conductivity, biocompatibility, and non-immunogenic properties, making it an outstanding candidate for bone repair []. Zn, another crucial element in the human body, provides a vital role in numerous physiological processes, such as bone metabolism. It promotes angiogenesis, osteogenic activity, and cell proliferation, while also maintaining a low toxicity profile [].
In the present study, it has been shown that the concentration of HAp-ZnO had an important influence on the viability of osteoblast cells. Specifically, when rosemary and thyme EOs were added to the HAp-ZnO composition, both oils were effective in reducing the cytotoxic effect of ZnO on the cells.
For instance, in the samples containing rosemary EO, cell viability exceeded 80% at a concentration of 25 μg/mL, regardless of whether the EO concentration was 2.5% or 5%. In the case of thyme EO, the concentration of the oil had a direct impact on cell viability. The HAp-ZnO-EO2C2 sample showed improved cell viability, with levels exceeding 80% at concentrations of 50 μg/mL and 25 μg/mL. By comparing the two EOs, it was noted that thyme EO was more effective in mitigating the cytotoxic effects of ZnO on cells than rosemary EO.
By comparing the two types of bacteria (Gram-positive and Gram-negative) it can be observed that Gram-negative bacteria is characterized by an extra special outer membrane (OM) that provide protection therefore this type of bacteria may be more resistant to antimicrobial therapies than Gram-positive bacteria which doesn’t have this important layer [].
Regarding the antimicrobial effects, it was observed that all the samples exhibited a similar inhibitory effect on Ps. aeruginosa, E. coli, and S. aureus. However, the materials demonstrated a very low antimicrobial effect against C. albicans. When comparing the two EOs, it was found that rosemary EO exhibited a stronger antimicrobial effect against the E. coli strain, whereas thyme EO was more effective against S. aureus.
Junka et al. synthesized a biocellulose carrier with thyme, clove and eucalyptus EOs with the purpose of eradicating staphylococcal and pseudomonal films. In the case of thyme EO, the results showed a high inhibition behavior for pseudomonal biofilm and a low effect for staphylococcal biofilm. One of the disadvantages this study provided was the low cell viability on fibroblast and osteoblast cell line in the case of thyme EO []. Thymol is described as a natural monoterpenoid phenol, being the main ingredient of oil obtained from Thymus vulgaris L. and other EOs []. Soumya et al. studied the action of carvacrol and thymol on Ps. aeruginosa and concluded that due to the hydrophilicity of these two components they may permit their diffusion through the polar polysaccharide matrix, while the hydrophobic properties of these two compounds may conduct to particular interactions with the antibacterial membrane []. Rosemary EO has extraordinary properties, such as antimicrobial, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, anti-cancer, antiviral and also it possesses great properties for neuronal activity [, ]. Jafari-sales & Pashazadeh studied the response of rosemary EO on S. aureus and E. coli by microdilution broth. The results showed that rosemary EO provides antimicrobial effect with an enhanced effect on Gram-positive bacteria [].
HAp is one of the most studied biomaterials regarding bone regeneration due to its properties such as bioactivity, osteoconductivity and biocompatibility []. One of the main disadvantages of HAp is its low mechanical strength and also may inhibit cell growth and produce cytotoxicity in high dosage, to overcome this disadvantage, metal ions such as silver (Ag), Zn, magnesium (Mg) and strontium (Sr) have been added in its composition []. HAp can be synthesized by many methods such as sol-gel [], co-precipitation [], solid state reaction [], hydrolysis [], and hydrothermal and others [], also its properties may be influenced by chemical composition, morphology and particle size []. Regarding the shape of HAp NPs, many research has shown that sphere-shaped NPs and nanorods are ideal for drug and gene delivery and cell targeting applications []. Hydrothermal synthesis represents a key method to obtain HAp by allowing the control of morphology by using temperature and pressure so different HAp NPs morphology may be obtained such as microsphere, sphere, rod, fiber, needle and many others [].
ZnO is a potent antibacterial agent that functions through a variety of methods involving several chemical species []. Zn possesses many antibacterial and antibiofilm properties altering a wide range of bacterial strains by interfering with many cell processes and inhibiting their growth and blocking the principal reactions in them []. Because Zn-doped biomaterials have the advantageous effect of speeding up tissue regeneration, they are frequently developed with biological features like biocompatibility, osseointegration, and osteoconductivity in mind [, ]. El-Masry et al. synthesized ZnO NPs with various dimensions and used them as antimicrobial agents against S. aureus, the study concluded that for a dimension of 20 nm the bacteria strain was better inhibited []. Valadbeigi et al. analyzed the effect of ZnO NPs on toxin–antioxin (TA) in Ps. aeruginosa. The study shown that ZnO NPs mazE was strongly expressed upon treatment with ZnO NPs, while mazF was suppressed; also, antitoxin higA was not expressed after the exposure to the ZnO NPs [].
Lala et al. studied the effect of Zn-doped carbonated HAp that was synthesized by mechanical alloying. They concluded that The Zn-doped A-cHAp NPs obtained by milling have an isotropic size and lattice strain and the crystalline A-cHAp lattice partially amorphizes as a result of the combined impact of Zn and mechanical alloying substitution in the HAp lattice []. Regarding the cell viability the undoped HAp sample and 5%, 10%, 15% and 20% mol Zn-doped A-cHAp provided about 90–95% for a concentration of 20 μg/mL, along with the concentration growth of sample the cell viability decreased to 80–85% at 100 μg/mL for all the samples []. de Lima et al. studied the effect of Zn-doped mesoporous HAp on S. aureus and E. coli, the samples containing Zn (0.5%, 1%, 2%) provided higher inhibitory effect on the bacteria strains compared to undoped HAp, as for the higher concentration (2% Zn) the inhibitory concentration was 50% for S. aureus and 77% for E. coli [].
## Conclusions
Based on the findings of this study, we successfully synthesized HAp-ZnO composites incorporating rosemary and thyme EOs using a microwave-assisted hydrothermal method. The characterization results confirmed that the composite materials exhibited two crystalline phases, both crystallizing in the hexagonal system. FT–IR analysis further validated the existence of characteristic functional groups from the EOs within the composite structure. Biological evaluations demonstrated that the incorporation of rosemary and thyme EOs significantly enhanced cell viability, particularly in reducing the cytotoxicity associated with ZnO. Notably, thyme EO showed superior effectiveness in promoting cell viability compared to rosemary EO. This suggests that these EOs can effectively mitigate the potential cytotoxic effects of ZnO in biomedical applications. Regarding antimicrobial activity, the synthesized composites exhibited a broad inhibitory effect against Ps. aeruginosa, E. coli, and S. aureus, with thyme EO showing greater efficacy against S. aureus and rosemary EO being more effective against E. coli. However, the composites displayed limited antimicrobial activity against C. albicans. HAp-ZnO composites enhanced with rosemary and thyme EOs offer promising potential for bone regeneration applications, combining improved biocompatibility with effective antimicrobial properties against common bacterial strains. Further research may be required to optimize these materials for broader antifungal applications.
