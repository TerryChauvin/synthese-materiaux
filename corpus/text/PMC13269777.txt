# In situ synthesis of ZIF-67 as sacrificial template within the sublayer of forward osmosis membrane to minimize internal concentration polarization
## Abstract
AbstractInternal concentration polarization (ICP) in the support layer significantly decreases the water flux performance of forward osmosis (FO) membranes. To overcome this, we developed a novel method for creating a highly porous support layer using ZIF-67 nanoparticles as a sacrificial template. The ZIF-67 nanoparticles were synthesized in a polymer casting solution at different precursor concentrations, including Co2+ and 2-methylimidazole. This method enhances nanoparticle dispersion in the support layer and enables scalable membrane fabrication. ZIF-67 nanoparticles are incorporated into the support layer matrix during phase inversion, then removed by immersing the ZIF-67-incorporated support layer in water, yielding a porous support layer. The modified support layer exhibited a porosity of 83%, which is 15% higher than that of the unmodified membrane, attributed to the templating effect of ZIF-67 nanoparticles. The performance of TFC-FO membranes was evaluated in a FO setup to correlate improved support layer characteristics with FO separation performance. The TFC-2 membrane (fabricated with 3.0 wt.% ZIF-67 precursors) exhibited water fluxes of 29.5 LMH (in FO mode) and 56.4 LMH (in PRO mode), representing a 2.3-fold increase compared to that of the control TFC membrane (13.2/24.6 LMH in FO/PRO modes, respectively). Additionally, the structural parameter decreased from 593 µm in TFC to 242 µm in TFC-2 membranes, indicating a substantial reduction in ICP. These results demonstrate that the in-situ synthesis of ZIF-67 as a sacrificial template is a simple and effective strategy for preparing high-porosity support layers with reduced ICP.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-49580-2.Keywords: Internal concentration polarization, Structural parameter, Support layer, Casting solution, Sacrificial template, ZIF-67Subject terms: Chemistry, Engineering, Materials science, Nanoscience and technology
## Introduction
Forward osmosis (FO) has recently garnered significant attention as an innovative separation technology, particularly in water treatment and desalination. Compared to conventional reverse osmosis (RO), FO offers several advantages, including lower capital costs, higher water recovery rates, and reduced membrane fouling, making it a promising alternative for sustainable water purification. In the FO process, water moves across a semipermeable membrane driven by the osmotic pressure gradient between the feed solution (FS, low salinity) and the draw solution (DS, high salinity). A widely used membrane in this process is the thin-film composite (TFC) membrane, considered the state of the art in asymmetric membrane design,. TFC membranes typically consist of two distinct layers: (1) a selective polyamide (PA) layer and (2) a porous support layer. The active PA layer is formed via interfacial polymerization (IP) of m-phenylenediamine (MPD) and trimesoyl chloride (TMC), yielding a cross-linked, thin layer with high selectivity. The support layer is fabricated using non-solvent-induced phase separation (NIPS), which provides mechanical stability for the PA active layer,. TFC membranes offer various advantages, including high chemical resistance, separation efficiency, and durability; however, their relatively low water permeability limits their broader application,. For a specific TFC membrane in the FO process, water flux is directly proportional to the osmotic pressure gradient across the PA active layer. However, a much lower water flux is usually observed in practice, primarily because the effective osmotic pressure gradient is lower than the theoretical one. This phenomenon is caused by concentration polarization (CP), one of the primary factors that reduces the effective osmotic pressure gradient and leads to a decline in water flux. Depending on where CP occurs, it can be classified into two types: internal concentration polarization (ICP) and external concentration polarization (ECP). In the FO process, ECP occurs at the surface of the PA or support layer, whereas ICP occurs within the pores of the support layer. When operating in FO mode (with the PA active layer facing the FS), water from the FS side permeates through the support layer, diluting the DS and causing dilutive ICP. Simultaneously, concentrative ECP occurs at the interface between the FS and PA layers. When operating in pressure retarded osmosis (PRO) mode (with the PA active layer facing the DS), water and solute diffuse from the FS bulk into the support layer, water passes through the PA layer while the solute accumulates inside the pores, leading to a concentrated ICP. Unlike ECP, which can be mitigated by controlling operating parameters, ICP occurs within the support layer, making it difficult to mitigate,. Therefore, numerous efforts have been made to mitigate ICP and its adverse impact on the FO process by modifying the support layer of FO membranes–. Specifically, a thin support layer with high porosity and low tortuosity improves water/salt diffusion within the membrane matrix, alleviating ICP. Recently, sacrificial materials have been introduced as an interesting method for creating a porous support layer with interconnected pores, known as a direct templating technique. In this method, templating nanomaterials are incorporated into the support layer structure and then removed under various conditions (acidic or alkaline) to construct porous membranes. Although different studies verified the efficiency of this method, some challenges restricted its application and development, including: (1) A nanomaterial or pore-forming template should be synthesized separately and then incorporated within the support layer by dispersing it in the membrane casting solution. (2) Nanomaterials also face the issue of agglomeration at high concentrations, which limits the percentage of nanomaterials that can be used. (3) In the template removal step, harsh conditions or reagents are required, which can damage the support layer structure or raise environmental concerns. Therefore, developing a method that can overcome these limitations would significantly enhance the effectiveness of the templating approach.
In this study, we synthesized a highly porous support layer via direct templating with ZIF-67 as the sacrificial template. The ZIF-67 was fabricated in situ by adding Co2 + and 2-methylimidazole (Hmim), the precursors for ZIF-67, to the casting solution. During the NIPS method, ZIF-67 is embedded within the support layer matrix. After 24 h of water immersion, ZIF-67 dissolved, creating nanovoids in the support layer and increasing its porosity. ZIF-67 concentrations were varied by different concentrations (but same ratio) of the Hmim/Co2+ to investigate their effect on support layer characteristics. The relationship between ZIF-67 concentration and the FO performance (water flux, reverse salt flux, and selectivity) of the prepared FO membranes is then studied. This method is practical, easy to operate, and significantly enhances the performance of TFC-FO membranes.
## Experiment section
## Materials
Polyethersulfone (PES, molecular weight: 58,000, BASF company) is utilized as the main polymer of the support layer, polyethylene glycol 400 (PEG, molecular weight = 400 g/mol) is used as a pore-forming agent, and n-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP) is used as a solvent to construct the support layers of the FO membranes. For depositing the PA active layer onto the support layers, 1,3-phenylenediamine (MPD, 99%) was used as a bifunctional amine monomer, and 1,3,5-benzenetricarbonyl trichloride (TMC, 98%) was used as a trifunctional acyl monomer. n-hexane (Merck) and DI water were used as TMC and MPD solvents, respectively. Additionally, cobalt nitrate hexahydrate (Co(NO3)2·6H2O, Merck) was used as a metal source, and 2-methylimidazole (Hmim, Merck) was employed as an organic ligand to synthesize the ZIF-67. NaCl (Iran mineral salt company) was used to prepare NaCl solutions for the FO and RO tests.
## Support layer fabrication
All support layers were fabricated via the NIPS process using a casting solution comprising a polymer, solvent, and pore-forming agent. A solution containing 1.3 g of PEG-400 in 4.3 g of NMP was prepared. Then, varying amounts of Co2+ and Hmim (at a constant Hmim/Co2+ molar ratio) were added to the solution. After 2 h of stirring at room temperature, 1.0 g of PES is added, and the solution is heated to 65 °C and stirred for 6 h to dissolve the PES. Upon ZIF-67 formation, a color transition to purple was observed. The chemical compositions of casting solutions are detailed in Table 1. The resulting solution was degassed, cast onto a pre-cleaned glass plate using a 100-µm-thick casting knife, and immediately immersed in a DI water coagulation bath to yield a purple PES nanocomposite support layer. Finally, the support layer was soaked in DI water for 24 h to leach out ZIF-67 nanoparticles, with the water refreshed every 2 h.

## PA active layer construction
The PA rejection layer was synthesized on the PES support layer via interfacial polymerization. In this process, MPD (2.0 wt.% aqueous solution) and TMC (0.1 wt.% in hexane) reacted at the organic-aqueous interface to form the PA layer. Initially, the top surface of the support layer was immersed in the MPD solution for 2 min. Excess MPD solution was subsequently removed using tissue paper. The MPD-saturated support was then contacted with the TMC-hexane solution for 1.5 min to initiate IP. After polymerization, the PA layer was rinsed with hexane to remove residual TMC monomers, then cured at 60 °C for 1 min to promote further polymerization. The resulting TFC-FO membranes were stored in DI water and labeled TFC-x, where x denotes the support layer code.
## Membrane characterization
Scanning electron microscopy (SEM; Tescan) was used to investigate the top, bottom, and cross-sectional morphologies of the FO membranes. Attenuated total reflection Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (ATR-FTIR, Bruker Equinox 55) was used to evaluate the chemical composition of the support and PA active layers. The elemental analysis of the PES-ZIF672 and PES-ZIF672-water support layers was assessed using the energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) method. Also, a water contact angle goniometer (WCA, Dataphysics, OCA 15 plus) was used to measure the hydrophilicity of the support layer surface at room temperature. The WCA was measured at five points on the membrane surface, and the average WCA was reported as the final data. The crystalline structures of the PES, PES-ZIF672, PES-ZIF672-water, and ZIF-67 powder (extracted from the PES-ZIF672 support layer) were investigated using X-ray diffraction (XRD; DX-2700BH). The surface roughness of the prepared support layers and the PA layer in the fabricated TFC membranes was investigated using an atomic force microscope (AFM, FemtoScan) in tapping mode. The porosity of the support layers was determined using the gravimetric method. In this procedure, three samples of each support layer were immersed in DI water for 24 h. ## Results and discussions
## Investigation of in situ synthesis of ZIF-67 nanoparticles
In this study, ZIF-67 nanoparticles were synthesized directly within the polymer casting solution to improve nanoparticle dispersion and establish a scalable, practical approach for direct templating methods. Various concentrations of ZIF-67 precursors, including Co2+ and Hmim, were dissolved in the polymer casting solution to synthesize ZIF-67. The resulting purple casting solution, containing different concentrations of ZIF-67, was then immersed in DI water to initiate the NIPS process and form a support layer embedded with ZIF-67 templates. The fabricated support layer was subsequently immersed in DI water for 24 h to leach out the embedded ZIF-67 particles, creating internal porosity. This method, which uses only water as the solvent, eliminates the need for chemical reagents, making it a more environmentally friendly, cost-effective, and scalable approach.
ZIF-67 can be synthesized using different solvents and additives, including polymers and surfactants,. This versatility highlights the adaptability of its synthesis method, enabling fine-tuning of particle characteristics, such as size, morphology, and surface functionality. However, to verify successful ZIF-67 synthesis in the casting solution and evaluate the medium’s effect on its physicochemical properties, ZIF-67 was separated from the support layer and analyzed using various characterization techniques. The PES-ZIF672 support layer was dissolved in DCM (before immersion in water for 24 h), and the nanoparticles were separated with a centrifuge and thoroughly washed to remove residual polymer. The separated ZIF-67 nanoparticles were characterized by XRD and SEM, as shown in Fig. 1a, b. Figure 1a confirms that ZIF-67 nanoparticles have an irregular octahedral morphology with a particle size in the range of 200–300 nm, indicating precise crystal growth and controlled nucleation during synthesis. The irregular surface features observed in the ZIF-67 morphology are believed to arise from interactions with the PES polymer coating the nanoparticle surfaces during crystallization.
XRD analysis confirmed the successful synthesis of ZIF-67 nanoparticles within the PES casting solution. As illustrated in Fig. 1b, the obtained diffraction pattern shows distinct Bragg peaks that closely match the simulated pattern of crystalline ZIF-67, confirming the preservation of its crystallinity during membrane fabrication. The sharp diffraction peaks observed at 2θ values of 7° (011), 12.5° (112), and 18° (222) are characteristic of the ZIF-67 crystal structure, further validating the formation of phase-pure nanocrystals,. The sharpness and intensity of these peaks indicate a high level of crystallinity and purity. This further supports the effectiveness of the ZIF-67 synthesis within the casting solution.
## Characterization of support layer
Fig. 2a presents the XRD patterns of PES, PES-ZIF672 (before ZIF-67 removal), and PES-ZIF672-Water (after water immersion, removing ZIF-67). The PES sample exhibited a broad peak at 2θ = 20°, characteristic of amorphous materials. However, in the PES-ZIF672 sample, distinct peaks corresponding to ZIF-67 are observed at 2θ values of 7° (011), 12.5° (112), and 18° (222), in addition to the broad amorphous characteristic of the PES matrix. These results confirm that ZIF-67 was successfully synthesized and incorporated into the PES support layer. This support layer was immersed in DI water for 24 h to remove ZIF-67 nanoparticles, forming the PES-ZIF672-Water membrane. As shown in the XRD pattern, the PES-ZIF672-Water sample exhibits only the characteristic broad peak of the PES polymer, with no detectable diffraction peaks corresponding to ZIF-67. This suggests that the ZIF-67 nanoparticles were completely removed during the water immersion step.
Figure 2b presents the ATR-FTIR spectra of ZIF-67 nanoparticles, bare PES, PES-ZIF672, and PES-ZIF672-Water support layers. In all membrane samples, the characteristic absorption bands of the PES matrix are observed. The 1246 cm-1 and 1150 cm-1 peaks correspond to the asymmetric and symmetric stretching vibrations of the sulfone (O = S = O) functional groups, respectively. Furthermore, the absorption bands at 1482 cm−1 and 1580 cm−1 are attributed to the stretching vibration of the aromatic rings of the PES backbone. In the ATR-FTIR spectrum of the PES-ZIF672 support layer, several additional peaks are observed alongside the characteristic bands of PES. These peaks are attributed to ZIF-67 nanoparticles and primarily arise from the organic ligand Hmim. The absorption band at 1660 cm−1 corresponds to the stretching vibration of the C = N bond, while the peaks at 2875 cm−1 and 2921 cm-1 are attributed to the imidazole ring’s C–H stretching vibrations. After immersing the support layer in water, the ATR-FTIR spectrum of the PES-ZIF672-Water sample showed no visible peaks associated with ZIF-67, confirming that the nanoparticles were removed completely.
## EDS-MAP
Elemental mapping analysis was conducted on the PES-ZIF672 support layer to assess the incorporation and distribution of ZIF-67 nanoparticles. This analysis provides detailed insights into how the ZIF-67 nanoparticles are dispersed and integrated within the support layer structure. As shown in Fig. 3a, the PES-ZIF672 membrane exhibits a distinctive deep purple color, visually confirming the presence of ZIF-67 nanoparticles within the membrane structure. Figure 3b, c show the elemental mapping image and EDS of the PES-ZIF672 support layer, revealing that the Co and N elements of ZIF-67 are uniformly distributed across the membrane surface. This uniform distribution indicates that the ZIF-67 nanoparticles are well dispersed throughout the structure, with no noticeable aggregation. After 24 h of immersion in DI water, the color of the membrane changed to white (Fig. 3d), confirming the removal of ZIF-67 nanoparticles from the support layer matrix. The EDS analysis of the PES-ZIF672-Water sample (Fig. 3e) shows a reduction in Co content. These findings are consistent with previous ATR-FTIR and XRD results, which verify the successful synthesis, incorporation, and removal of ZIF-67 nanoparticles.
## Characterization of the direct templating method on support layer characteristics
## Porosity
The support layer’s porosity is a key factor that affects ICP intensity during the FO process. Higher porosity can reduce the severity of ICP, thereby improving water flux in the FO process. As shown in Fig. 4a, the porosity of the sacrificial ZIF-67-modified support layers increased. The bare PES support layer exhibited the lowest porosity (75.5%), whereas the PES-ZIF673 membrane showed the highest porosity (84.5%). This significant increase in porosity is attributed to the sacrificial behavior of ZIF-67 nanoparticles. When the embedded nanoparticles are removed, they leave behind a network of interconnected voids throughout the support structure, greatly enhancing its porosity. This process effectively creates a highly porous support layer, which can improve the performance of the FO membrane.
## PWP
The PWP measurements of support layers were conducted using DI water as the feed solution under an applied pressure of 2.0 bar; further experimental details are provided in the supporting information. As shown in Fig. 4a), the PWP values increase with increasing ZIF-67 content in the support layer. The PES-ZIF673-Water membrane showed a PWP of 265.9 LMH/bar, 2.7 times that of the bare PES support layer with a PWP of 98.1 LMH/bar. This significant enhancement in PWP is primarily due to increased porosity in the modified support layers, resulting from the removal of ZIF-67 nanoparticles. Removing the nanoparticles creates a network of interconnected voids within the support layer matrix, facilitating water transport by providing additional pathways. The average surface pore size of the support layer is a crucial factor in determining its permeability and, in turn, the subsequent formation of the PA active layer. Larger pore sizes can enhance water permeability by enabling more efficient transport and influencing the uniformity of the PA active layer during formation. This relationship is essential for optimizing the overall performance of FO membranes. As illustrated in Fig. 4b, the mean surface pore size increases from 14.1 nm for the pristine PES to 23.2 nm for the PES-ZIF673-Water support layer. This enhancement in pore size can be attributed to two factors: (1) the altered phase separation kinetics during the NIPS process due to the presence of ZIF-67 nanoparticles, and (2) the direct templating effect of ZIF-67.
## Support layer hydrophilicity
The surface hydrophilicity of the support layer is evaluated using the WCA analysis, as shown in Fig. 4b. The pristine PES support layer exhibited the highest WCA value of 77.6°, indicating the lowest hydrophilicity due to its inherently hydrophobic aromatic structure. However, the WCA of the ZIF-67-templated support layer decreased significantly, reaching 65.3° for the PES-ZIF673-Water membrane. The increase in surface hydrophilicity can be attributed to the increase in surface roughness and porosity resulting from the sacrificial role of ZIF-67 nanoparticles.
## Morphology
The AFM and SEM analysis of the top surface of the PES support layer (Figs. 5 and 6a) revealed a smooth morphology with no visible ZIF-67 nanoparticles. As shown in Fig. 5, the surface roughness of the ZIF-67-modified support layer is higher than that of the control PES support layer; however, a higher ZIF-67 loading slightly affects surface roughness. The enhanced surface roughness could be attributed to ZIF-67 nanoparticles deposited on the top surface during NIPS, resulting in a rougher surface. After water immersion, the nanoparticles were removed, but the top-surface topology remained unchanged. From the SEM images, the pores formed by the direct templating approach were not visible at this magnification. However, PWP measurements and the calculated average surface pore size confirmed an increase in pore size in the support layers prepared via sacrificial templating. Furthermore, no breakage or defects were observed on the support layers’ surfaces after ZIF-67 removal. Since the PA layer forms on the top surface of the support layer, these results indicate that the templating method will not negatively affect the synthesis of the PA layer during the IP process. While the top surface exhibited a dense structure with nanoscale pores, as shown in Fig. 6b, SEM analysis revealed notable morphological changes on the bottom side of the support layers. All support layers exhibited an open, porous bottom surface; however, the PES-ZIF673-Water membrane had a significantly more open structure, characterized by larger pores and higher pore density. Since the bottom side faces the FS/DS, higher porosity in this surface enhances salt and water diffusion, effectively reducing ICP.
The SEM images in Fig. 6c provide valuable insight into the cross-sectional morphology of the support layers. The support layers have an asymmetric structure, with a dense upper region and a porous lower region. This asymmetric morphology is a characteristic feature of the NIPS process. All support layers feature finger-like channels that extend from the top to the bottom across the membrane width. The ZIF-67-templated PES support layers exhibit broader finger-like pores than bare PES supports. This difference is likely due to the decreased NMP-water exchange rate during the NIPS process, which allows for the formation of larger, more open channels in the ZIF-67-templated support layers. The PES-ZIF672-Water sublayer exhibited straighter finger-like channels, which can reduce tortuosity. Additionally, the pore walls of the ZIF-67-templated support layers were thinner than those of the bare PES substrate. Magnified SEM images (Fig. 6d) show numerous pores on both the surfaces and walls of the channels, which result from the removal of the ZIF-67 template. These pores are about 200–300 nm, which matches the size of the ZIF-67 nanoparticles separated from the support layer structure. It is worth noting that some pores are larger (400–600 nm), which may be attributed to the slight agglomeration of ZIF-67 nanoparticles. These voids form an interconnected pore network that enhances salt diffusion, improving the overall performance of the support layer in FO membranes. In contrast, magnified SEM images of the bare PES support (Fig. 6e) showed no surface pores, confirming the proposed.
mechanism for the PES-ZIF672-Water membrane. However, in the PES-ZIF673-Water sample, the finger-like morphology became looser again, accompanied by increased membrane tortuosity. This change is likely due to a rise in the viscosity of the casting solution.
## Characterization of TFC membranes
Since the PA active layer is crucial for membrane selectivity, examining how modifications to the support layer influence its surface characteristics is important. Specifically, removing ZIF-67 through water immersion could affect the formation and properties of the PA layer during interfacial polymerization. The successful synthesis of the PA layer was confirmed by ATR-FTIR analysis (Fig. S1, supporting information), indicating that removing ZIF-67 does not hinder the polymerization process. However, the morphological changes in the PA active layer were analyzed using SEM images. As shown in Fig. 7a, all TFC-FO membranes exhibit the ridge-and-valley morphology typical of PA layers formed by interfacial polymerization. Particularly, TFC membranes prepared on ZIF-67-modified support layers display more pronounced PA protuberances than those fabricated on an unmodified support layer. Additionally, the thickness and roughness of the PA layer in modified membranes are increased, as verified by cross-section SEM and 3D AFM images (Fig. 7b, c). This observation suggests that the modified support layer influences the interfacial polymerization process. The PA layer forms at the immiscible interface between MPD and TMC solutions. In ZIF-67-modified PES support layers, the larger pores retain more MPD within the matrix. These monomers diffuse into the organic phase during interfacial polymerization, increasing the local MPD concentration at the interface. This higher concentration enhances polymerization, resulting in a thicker, rougher PA layer,. Additionally, the larger surface pore size facilitates faster, easier diffusion of MPD monomers into the organic phase, increasing monomer concentration at the reaction interface. However, as shown, the thickness and roughness of the PA layer in the TFC-3 membrane are reduced. During interfacial polymerization, several factors influence the final characteristics of the PA layer. Although a larger pore size facilitates greater diffusion of MPD monomers into the organic phase, it also increases the sorption of amine monomers within the pores. The higher availability of MPD results in the formation of a dense nascent PA thin film when the TMC organic solution contacts the support layer. This dense initial film subsequently restricts further diffusion of amine monomers. The trade-off between these parameters ultimately governs the final structure of the PA thin film in TFC-3 membranes.
## Intrinsic transport properties and structural parameters
The performance of TFC-FO membranes was assessed using a dead-end RO setup at an external pressure of 5 bar to determine the intrinsic parameters, including the water permeability coefficient (A), the salt permeability coefficient (B), and the salt rejection (Rs). The details of the experiment are provided in the supporting information. As listed in Table 2, the unmodified TFC membrane exhibited a relatively low A value of 1.25 LMH/bar. In comparison, ZIF-67 modification significantly improved membrane performance, with the TFC-2 membrane exhibiting the highest A value of 2.5 LMH/bar, a two-fold increase over the unmodified membrane. This improvement is attributed to the increased surface roughness of the PA active layer, as proved by SEM and AFM analyses. The improved surface roughness of the PA layer increases the membrane’s effective area, thereby increasing the A value. The ZIF-67-modified TFC membranes also exhibit increased salt rejection and salt permeability, and a reduced B/A ratio relative to the unmodified membrane. According to Eq. (S5), salt permeability is directly proportional to the water permeability coefficient and inversely proportional to the membrane rejection. Therefore, B is expected to increase substantially when A increases significantly, even if Rs increases only slightly. The high salt rejection and selectivity of the TFC-2 membrane can be attributed to the uniform formation of the PA-selective layer, which is facilitated by the optimized interfacial polymerization reaction, driven by the support layer’s optimal surface characteristics.

## FO performance
The performance of bare and modified TFC membranes in FO was systematically analyzed, as illustrated in Fig. 8a, b. As shown, the control TFC membrane has a water flux of 13.2 LMH in FO mode and 24.6 LMH in PRO mode. However, after structural modification with ZIF-67 sacrificial nanoparticles, a significant improvement in water flux was observed, consistent with the A-value trends in Table 2. At an optimum concentration, the TFC-2 membrane exhibits the highest water fluxes of 29.4 LMH in FO mode and 56.4 LMH in PRO mode, respectively, more than 2 times that of the control TFC membrane. The observed enhancement in water flux can be attributed to synergistic modifications in the support and active layers of the TFC membrane. For the support layer, two key structural improvements contribute to this performance enhancement: (1) an optimized structure featuring finger-like morphology with interconnected pores, which reduces tortuosity, and (2) significantly increased porosity, which mitigates ICP. By alleviating ICP, the osmotic pressure gradient across the active layer increases, thereby enhancing water flux. The increased surface roughness of the PA layer in the modified TFC-FO membranes enhances the membrane’s effective surface area, facilitating greater interaction with water molecules. However, further increasing the ZIF-67 loading in the PES-ZIF673-Water support reduced water flux, likely due to increased casting solution viscosity, which adversely affected the support-layer morphology. Reverse salt flux is a crucial performance metric for evaluating membrane efficiency in the FO process. As shown in Fig. 8b, the reverse salt flux also increases with an increase in water flux due to the intrinsic permeability-selectivity trade-off, which is consistent with previous studies,.
Specific salt flux, defined as the Js/Jw ratio, is another key metric for FO membranes. A lower value indicates a smaller Js relative to Jw, thereby minimizing solute leakage from DS to FS. As shown in Fig. 9a, the ZIF-67 templated TFC membranes exhibit a significant decline in specific salt flux compared to the unmodified TFC membrane. For instance, the Js/Jw values of the bare TFC membrane were 0.2 g/L in FO mode, which reduced to 0.12 g/L in the TFC-2 membrane, indicating enhanced membrane selectivity.
The effect of DS concentration on the water flux of TFC and TFC-2 membranes is presented in Fig. 9b. As shown, the water flux increases as DS concentration rises from 0.5 to 1.5 M. However, the increase in water flux is more pronounced for the TFC-2 membrane than for the control TFC membrane, because higher DS concentration intensifies the ICP effect. The TFC-2 membrane, with its modified support layer, can effectively mitigate ICP, thereby increasing the effective osmotic driving force and improving water flux.
To further assess the efficacy of our ZIF-67 direct template approach (in situ synthesis and removal), we compared the FO performance of the fabricated TFC-FO membranes with that reported in prior studies. As summarized in Table 3, the template-assisted membranes developed in this work demonstrate higher water flux and selectivity performance than previously reported membranes with similar design strategies. A distinct advantage of our approach is the use of water as an environmentally benign and cost-effective template-removal agent, compared with conventional methods that typically require hazardous acidic solutions (e.g., HCl/HF), which pose both safety and scalability concerns. Moreover, the in situ synthesis of ZIF-67 eliminates the need for separate nanoparticle preparation and integration steps, thereby simplifying the fabrication process. Overall, the results demonstrate that this technique offers an efficient, scalable, and environmentally friendly approach to customizing the support layer structure in high-performance TFC-FO membranes.

## Conclusion
In this study, sacrificial ZIF-67 nanoparticles were used as a sacrificial template within PES support layers to fabricate high-performance TFC-FO membranes. The ZIF-67 nanoparticles were synthesized in situ within the casting solution and subsequently removed by immersion in water, resulting in a highly porous support layer with interconnected pores. Characterization techniques, including XRD, FTIR, SEM, and EDS mapping, confirmed the successful formation of ZIF-67 and its subsequent removal from the membrane structure. The modified support layers exhibited significantly improved morphology, porosity, pure water permeability, and mean pore size. At optimum ZIF-67 concentration, the TFC-2 membrane shows a water permeability of 2.5 LMH/bar in the RO and a water flux of 29.4 LMH in the FO process, which was more than 2 times that of the control TFC membrane with 1.25 LMH/bar in the RO process and 13.2 in the FO process. This enhancement was due to improved sublayer support layer porosity, which decreased the structure parameter and alleviated ICP. Overall, this work confirms that the in situ synthesis of ZIF-67 nanoparticles as a sacrificial template and pore-forming agent offers a promising, scalable strategy to enhance the properties of support layers, thereby enabling the fabrication of high-performance TFC-FO membranes with significantly reduced ICP.

Experiment section
PWP measurement
The pure water permeability (PWP) of the support layers was evaluated using a laboratory-scale cross-flow filtration system with an effective membrane area of 4.9 cm2. DI water was used as the feed solution, and the operating pressure was adjusted to 2.0 bar during the test. Before PWP measurements, the system was pre-conditioned at 3.0 bar for 20 minutes to stabilize the permeation flux. All PWP measurements were performed in triplicate at room temperature, and the mean values are reported. The PWP values were calculated according to Eq. 1:
PWP = mwΔP×ρw×Am×Δt (1)
Where mw is the permeated water weight, 𝛒w is the water density at room temperature, and ∆P represents the applied pressure.
The average pore diameter (rp, nm) of the support layer was subsequently determined using the Guerout-Elford-Ferry equation (Eq. (2)):
rm=(2.9-1.75ε)×8QηTε
| (2)
|
Where Q (m3/s) represents the flow of pure water at applied external pressure (∆P), T (cm) indicates the thickness of the support layer, and η (Pa·s) signifies the viscosity of water at room temperature.
RO test
A dead-end RO testing setup was employed to measure pure water permeability (A, LMH/bar), solute permeability (B, LMH), and NaCl rejection (Rs, %). The effective filtration area of the membrane cell was 3.14 cm², and the temperature during testing was maintained at 25 °C. DI water was used as the feed solution during the RO test to calculate the A value. All parameters (A, B, and Rs values) were measured at a pressure of 5 bar. The A value is calculated by Eq. 3:
A=ΔVpΔt×Am×ΔP (3)
Here, Δt (h) indicates the time of RO assessment, ΔP (bar) represents the hydraulic pressure, and ΔVp (L) is the volume of permeated water.
For the Rs calculation, a feed solution of 1000 ppm NaCl was used. The Rs value is determined using Eq. 4:
Rs=(1-CpCf )×100 % (4)
Here, Cp refers to the concentration of salt in the permeate solution, while Cf denotes the concentration of salt in the feed solution.
Taking into account the parameters R, ΔP, A, and Δπ (osmotic pressure of the FS, in bar), the value of B was calculated using the following equation:
1-RR=BA(ΔP-Δπ) (5)
The S value (μm) of the TFC membranes in the FO mode was determined using a combination of RO and FO data according to the following equation:
S=DsJVlnAπD-Jw+BAπF+B
| (6)
|
Where D represents the bulk diffusion coefficient of aqueous NaCl, Jw represents the water flux in the FO mode, πF is the FS osmotic pressure, and πD is the DS bulk osmotic pressure.
Results and discussion
ATR-FTIR spectrum of the support layers
The ATR-FTIR spectra were collected for the support layers over a wavenumber range of 600 to 3900 cm-1 as shown in Fig. S1(a). The FTIR spectra of all samples display several characteristic peaks of PES. Specifically, absorption bands in the wavenumber range of 1245 to 1154 cm-1 correspond to stretching vibrations of the O=S=O functional group, while peaks at 1485 and 1581 cm-1 are associated with the vibrations of the benzene group in the PES polymer chain [1]. Notably, no new peaks appear when the PES-ZIF-67 support layers were immersed in DI water, which is attributed to the removal of ZIF-67 nanoparticles.
ATR-FTIR spectroscopy, a widely used analytical technique for assessing surface chemical compositions, was employed in this study to examine the formation and structural changes of the PA active layer on sacrificial ZIF-67-modified support layers in TFC-FO membranes (Fig. S1(b)). The spectral analysis revealed distinct vibrational bands at 1538 cm⁻¹ (C–N stretching, amide II), 1610 cm⁻¹ (N–H bending), and 1667 cm⁻¹ (C=O stretching, amide I), confirming the successful IP process [2]. Notably, the characteristic peaks of the PES support layer appeared in these spectra due to the penetration depth of the ATR-FTIR. Compared to the TFC membrane, no new peaks emerged for the ZIF-67-modified membranes, likely due to the removal of ZIF-67 nanoparticles during the water immersion.
