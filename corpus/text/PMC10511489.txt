# Ultrasonic-pretreated hydrothermal synthesis of less dense zeolite CHA from the transformation of zeolite T
## Abstract
Graphical abstract
## Introduction
Zeolites are important inorganic aluminosilicate materials with three-dimensional crystalline structure containing well-defined channels and cavities , . Abundant pore sizes, tunable Si/Al ratios and diverse microscopic morphologies endow zeolites many inherent properties, like molecular sieve, hydrophilicity, ion exchange capacity, and acid stability. Therefore, with their unique performance in catalytic reaction, permeability, and adsorption process and so on, zeolites have been widely used in industrial and agricultural fields , , , , , .
Recently, interzeolite conversion which is an alternative strategy to synthesize zeolite has attracted extensive attention , , , , . One particular advantage of interzeolite transformation over conventional hydrothermal synthesis is the crystallization rate. The initial zeolite decomposes into secondary structural units and then assembles into another zeolite under appropriate conditions. The crystallization process from the parent zeolite to the target topology can be accelerated via interzeolite transition with the help of kinetic control . When considering interzeolite transformation, each pair of parent-daughter crystals usually share similar structural units, such as common composite building unit (CBU), secondary building units (SBU) or ring building units (RBU). Furthermore, the synchronization between the depolymerization of original zeolite and the generation of target zeolite should be guaranteed by the optimization of the synthetic parameters, especially alkalinity, recrystallization temperature and time .
Zeolite CHA (Chabazite) is a promising small-pore zeolite. Its structure is made of a double 6-ring (D6R) as the basic building and its large CHA cavity is accessed by six eight-ring windows measuring around 0.38 nm × 0.38 nm , . Due to its 3-dimensional interconnected pore system with 8-ring windows (0.38 nm × 0.38 nm), zeolite CHA has received particular attention as a versatile catalyst and adsorbent in many processes such as conversion of methanol or ethanol into light olefins (MTO), selective catalytic reduction (SCR) of NOx, separation membrane for the dehydration of aqueous organic solutions and gas separations , , , , , . Zeolite CHA has been synthesized through interzeolite transformation using LEV , FAU , and MFI zeolites as the original crystals. In this work, zeolite CHA has been recrystallized from zeolite T which is an intergrowth-type zeolite of erionite (ERI) and offretite (OFF) with effective pore size of 0.36 nm × 0.51 nm . Due to the similarity of ERI and OFF structures which are alternately composed of ε-cage and double 6-ring (D6R), they always exist together as crystallized products. Because of stacking faults of erionite sheets on offretite phase, the effective pore size of zeolite T is as same as that of erionite although offretite is the main phase , .
The same secondary building units (D6R) can provide theoretical support that zeolite CHA can be prepared from interzeolite conversion of zeolite T. Zhou et al. reported zeolite CHA seeds in yielding zeolite CHA membrane were directly prepared from aluminosilicate gel with the addition of a little amount of nanosized zeolite T. However, the detailed investigation of interconversion from zeolite T to zeolite CHA has not been found. In this paper, the pure zeolite CHA has been synthesized with low temperature of 353 K without using any aluminosilicate gel just simply alkali-treated. Furthermore, with the ultrasonic assistance, the particle sizes of CHA crystals can be reduced to about 1 μm. As well known, ultrasound can produce more effective homogeneous nucleation function and process intensification in the preparation of zeolites , , . Combination of ultrasonic treatment and hydrothermal synthesis, the recrystallization rate from the starting zeolite T to the final zeolite CHA has been greatly improved and size of final product has been effectively decreased. Zeolite CHA synthesized with ultrasonic treatment has displayed higher CO2 adsorption ability compared to the sample without ultrasonic treatment.
Thermodynamic stability of zeolite decreases with lowering framework density and transformation of thermodynamically stable zeolite into thermodynamically less stable zeolite is not possible from the thermodynamic viewpoint. However, the possibility of transforming zeolite into less dense zeolites has already been reported. Goto et al. reported the interzeolite conversion of less dense CHA-type zeolite (FDSi = 15.1 T/1000 Å3) from LEV-type zeolite with the framework density (FDSi) of 15.9 T/1000 Å3 . They think that it is possible to observe crystallization of CHA-type zeolite in a kinetically controlled process if CHA phase is a metastable state during transformation towards the most stable state. The discovery in our experiment has approved this opinion and the production of less dense zeolite CHA (FDSi = 15.1 T/1000 Å3) from zeolite T (FDSi = 16.1 T/1000 Å3) has been realized. To our knowledge, this is the first report on the transformation of the pure zeolite CHA from zeolite T through simply hydrothermal synthesis with ultrasonic assistance in alkaline solution. In order to obtain the pure zeolite CHA with small size, the ultrasonic temperature and time have been thoroughly investigated.
## Experimental
## Synthesis of the parent zeolite T
Zeolite T was chosen as the parent zeolite and the synthetic process was similar with that of our previous report and made slight modification. The precursor suspension had the following molar composition: 1SiO2: 0.025Al2O3: 0.15Na2O: 0.15K2O: 0.06TMAOH: 15H2O. 0.62 g NaOH (96%, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co.Ltd.) and 0.98 g KOH (85%, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co.Ltd.) were dissolved in the distilled water at first, and then 1.09 g TMAOH (25%, Cairui Chemical Reagent Co.Ltd.) was slowly added to this alkali solution at room temperature. After that, 0.06 g Al powder (99%, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co.Ltd.) was added very slowly to this solution under magnetic stirring, this process should be maintained about 2 h to make sure that Al source was dissolved completely in the solution. Subsequently, 7.5 g colloidal silica (40% of SiO2, Shandong Haiyang Chemical Co. Ltd.) was added drop by drop to this solution very slowly to prevent the agglomeration in the mixture. After homogenized by vigorous magnetic stirring for 6 h and statically aged for 12 h, the milk-like precursor was transferred to stainless steel autoclave and kept at 353 K for 4 days and 393 K for 4 days under static hydrothermal crystallization. The synthesized samples were washed, filtered and dried at 373 K overnight. The final products were obtained by heating 873 K for 6 h under air condition in the muffle.
## Interzeolite transformation of the products
At first, 0.96 g MOH (KOH or NaOH) was slowly dissolved in 10 g the distilled water. Secondly, 1 g zeolite T seeds were slowly added to this aqueous caustic solution and the mixture keeps stirring for 2 h at room temperature. Then, the solution was transferred into the stainless-steel autoclave with Teflon liner and reacted in the oven temperature of 353 K for the different time. The final product was washed, filtered and dried after the recrystallization. In order to investigate the influence of ultrasonic treatment on the zeolite transformation, the seeds precursor was ultrasonic treated for 2 h or 4 h, and the ultrasonic temperature was settled at 313 K or 333 K before hydrothermal synthesis. The energy input of ultrasound equipment (Jielite Ultrasound Technology) was 200 W and the ultrasound produced acoustic waves at the frequency of 40 kHz. In order to explore the detailed progress of interzeolite transformation, the NaOH-treated sample with ultrasound temperature of 313 K was conducted a time- tracking experiment. ## Products characterization
The crystallites of samples were observed by XRD with Rigaku-Dmax 2400 diffractometer. The morphology and composition of samples were checked using SEM and EDX analysis with Quanta 250 instrument. Single-component adsorption isotherms for N2 and CO2 were measured with Micromeritics 3Flex analyzer. FT-IR spectra of samples were detected by the FTIR-850 spectrometer and KBr was dried overnight in an oven prior to analysis. The ICP result of the products was measured by an Agilent 7900 ICP-MS.
Solid-state 27Al and 29Si Magic Angle Spin Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (MAS NMR) spectra were recorded by Bruker Avance 400 MHz spectrometer using a 4 mm diameter zirconia rotor. 27Al MAS NMR measurement was carried at spinning rate of 14 kHz and pulse width of 1.2 μs (45°). 29Si MAS NMR measurement was carried at spinning rate of 12 kHz and pulse width of 1.6 μs (30°). Pyridine infrared (Py-IR) spectra were obtained on a Nicolet iS50 FT-IR Spectrometer, and the details are as follows. Samples were pressed into round disk, vacuum pretreated at 400 °C for 1 h, cooled to room temperature, imported pyridine vapor and adsorbed for 20 min, and then desorption of pyridine was carried out at 150, 300 and 400 °C, respectively. Finally, spectra were recorded after the temperature decreased to room temperature.
## Results and discussions
## Zeolite transformation in KOH-treated solution
SEM image of the parent zeolite T has been displayed in Fig. 1a. The particles show nano-crystals aggregates with overall globular-like shape from the observations of SEM image. Its XRD result (Fig. 1b) shows the clear peaks of the typical zeolite T framework. Zeolite T is an intergrowth-type zeolite of erionite (ERI) and offretite (OFF), in which offretite is the main phase, therefore the XRD pattern of zeolite T shows the characteristic peaks of OFF phase. The SEM image of the daughter product prepared by 48 h at 353 K without the ultrasonic treatment in KOH solution (Sample 1) has been displayed in Fig. 1c. The particles with the size of about 5 μm present rough walnut-like shape, which is the basic morphology of CHA phase. The XRD result shown in Fig. 1d proves that the product is zeolite CHA with the relatively uneven baseline, which indicates that sample 1 has rather lower crystallinity. It illustrates that the synthetic route of zeolite CHA from zeolite T is feasible, however the condition of recrystallization should be further explored.
Fig. 2 presents XRD results of sample 2 to sample 7 with the different ultrasonic condition in KOH solution. Sample 2 with the condition of the ultrasound of 2 h at 313 K and crystallization of 48 h at 353 K is free from impurities referring to standard CHA phase pattern, which indicates ultrasonic pretreatment can effectively remove MER phase. For sample 3, no diffraction peaks other than those of CHA topology have not been observed when prolonging the ultrasonic time to 4 h at 313 K. If the crystallization time increased from 48 h to 96 h, it can be found that sample 4 has a lower CHA peaks and the zeolite W (framework code MER, merlinoite framework topology) has been detected (marked with △). This result can imply that the overlong crystallization time easily stimulates the emergence of the mixed crystals. It has been reported that MER phase is liable to be co-crystallized during the preparation of zeolite CHA , . The formation of CHA and MER frameworks are competing with each other when K+ used in the precursor. MER phase with higher framework density (FDSi = 16.4 T/1000 Å3) takes precedence over CHA phase (FDSi = 15.1 T/1000 Å3) to be produced because of its higher hydrothermal stability, and it is very difficult to avoid the generation of MER during the synthesis of zeolite CHA. Increasing the ultrasonic temperature to 333 K, the different consequence has been distinctly found. The subtle MER peaks have been arisen for sample 5 to sample 7 (The corresponding synthetic conditions have been listed in Table 1). The above XRD results clearly confirm that the synthetic range of zeolite CHA is very narrow and the completing MER phase with higher hydrothermal stability is liable to be coexisted. It has been found that the condition of ultrasonic temperature is an important factor for the transformation of zeolite T into pure CHA phase.
Fig. 3 shows the morphologies of sample 2 to sample 7, which are well keeping with the XRD results of Fig. 2. Fig. 3a and b reveal that the crystals of sample 2 and sample 3 are the distinct walnut-like pellets with the size of 2 ∼ 3 μm, which is the typical shape of micro-sized zeolite CHA . For sample 4 to sample 7, A few particles of zeolite MER with the dumbbell shape have been mixed in CHA type crystals (marked with rectangle in Fig. 3c-f), which is consistent with the normal morphology of zeolite MER in the literature . The crystallization of zeolite CHA is a kinetically controlled process and CHA is a relatively open framework among the competitors, hence it is an instable one which is difficult to crystallize and easy to transform . Combined the XRD results and SEM images of products in KOH solution, it can be concluded that the ultrasonic treatment of 313 K is better than that of 333 K, the appropriate ultrasonic condition can not only decrease the size of crystals but also improve the purity of the products.
## Zeolite transformation in NaOH-treated solution
The XRD diffractograms of products prepared from NaOH solution have been displayed in Fig. 4. Sample 8 without the ultrasound process shows the pure CHA phase. For samples 9 and 10 with ultrasonic pretreatment of 2 h and 4 h at 313 K, the positions of peaks are consistent with the standard pattern of zeolite CHA. However, the heights of characteristic peaks of sample 10 are a little lower than those of sample 9, which indicates that prolonging the ultrasonic time from 2 h to 4 h cannot efficiently improve the degree of crystallinity. XRD pattern of sample 11 with the ultrasound of 2 h at 333 K and recrystallization of 48 h at 353 K reveals small reflection at 28.1° of zeolite P (marked with ◆), which belong to gismondine (GIS) framework topology. GIS is a kind of 8-membered small pore zeolite which is usually formed during the hydrothermal transformation of FAU in aqueous solutions of NaOH , . Whereas, the impure peaks of sample 12 with ultrasonic treatment of 4 h at 333 K and recrystallization of 48 h at 353 K have disappeared and its crystallinity degree of CHA phase is a little higher than that of sample 11. From the XRD analysis of sample 13, the diffraction pattern of GIS impurity has been increased with recrystallization time of 96 h.
Observed by SEM images of sample 8 without the ultrasonic treatment in Fig. 5a, the walnut-like CHA crystals with average particle size of about 2 ∼ 4 μm have been formed in crystallization time of 48 h. With the ultrasonic assistance of 313 K, samples 9 and 10 (Fig. 5b and c) show more well-defined CHA morphology and smaller particle size (about 1 μm) than sample 8, which demonstrates that the ultrasound can significantly provide acoustic cavitation and process intensification , , . Increasing the irradiation time from 2 h to 4 h has no significant effect on the size of particles from the observation of Fig. 5b and c. However, with the ultrasonic treatment of 2 h at 333 K, impurity of zeolite P (GIS) has been shown in Fig. 5d for sample 11 (marked with square). The pure walnut-like CHA crystals (sample 12, Fig. 5e) have been displayed when changing the ultrasonic time from 2 h to 4 h at 333 K. If increasing the synthetic time from 48 h to 96 h, more stable GIS phase can be clearly detected from the SEM image of sample 13 in Fig. 5f. The framework density of GIS (FDSi = 16.4 T/1000 Å3) is higher than that of CHA (FDSi = 15.1 T/1000 Å3), therefore, the zeolite crystallization process is kinetically stable towards denser GIS framework structures.
The synthesis condition which favors decomposition of the original zeolite and rearrangement of the target structure is very important, which should be thoroughly investigated . From the observation of the above results, it can be discovered that both KOH and NaOH can play the structure directing role in the interzeolite conversion process. The final products are greatly affected by alkalinity and additional alkali metal ions. The synthesis condition should be carefully controlled because that impurity is easily coexisted. In our experiment, MER phase is inclined to be the by-product in KOH-treated solution and GIS phase is tend to be mixed in NaOH-treated solution. For a successful interzeolite transformation to the target topology, the process must be thermodynamically and kinetically favorable. The thermodynamic approach assumes that zeolites are metastable and thus evolve from a less dense to a denser phase, following Ostwald’s rule . Both the main phase (OFF phase) and the minor phase (ERI phase) of zeolite T have the same framework density (FDSi = 16.1 T/1000 Å3), however, the CHA phase has a lower framework density (FDSi = 15.1 T/1000 Å3), which can be considered as metastable intermediate and the reaction is inclined to form more stable phase of MER phase (FDSi = 16.4 T/1000 Å3) or GIS phase (FDSi = 16.4 T/1000 Å3).
## Structure and acid property of samples
Fig. 6 displays results of Rietveld refinement for samples 8 and 9, revealing that the XRD patterns are in well agreement with CHA structure (JCPDS PDF#19-1178). No reflections attributed to the impurities were found in the obtained zeolite. The lattice parameters of samples 8 and 9 obtained from Rietveld refinement have been depicted in Table S1. Based on our XRD data and the formulation in reference of , the lattice parameters of samples 1, 2, 8 and 9 have been calculated and the detailed results have been presented in Table S2. The lattice parameters obtained from our experiment have no significant changes with those from the reference of . The yields and relative crystallinities of samples 1, 2, 8 and 9 have been shown in Table S2. The yields of products almost keep the constant value of about 40% and sample 8 has the highest crystallinity intensity among the four samples.
The 29Si MAS NMR spectra of samples 1, 2, 8 and 9 have been shown in Fig. 7, it can be observed that the peaks of all samples appear in the range of −87 ppm to −109 ppm, and the five peaks can be rationally assigned as Si(4Al), Si(3Al), Si(2Al), Si(1Al) and Si(0Al) , demonstrating that the samples exhibit diverse silicon species. The silicon species are well incorporated in the zeolite framework with different coordinated aluminum atoms and the existence of the Si(4Al) species indicates that the obtained products have a high aluminum content. Fractions of Si species in synthesized samples calculated by deconvoluting the 29Si NMR spectra have been shown in Fig. S1. The frameworks of aluminum in the samples 1, 2, 8 and 9 have been analyzed by 27Al NMR, and the spectral results are shown in Fig. 8. All the samples show resonance of chemical shift at around 60 ppm, corresponding to the coordination of Al tetrahedral in the framework, and no resonance at 0 ppm is observed, indicating that there is not octahedral coordination of aluminum. As indicated by the solid-state 27Al MAS NMR spectra, aluminum atoms in structures of these four samples are incorporated in the zeolite framework with the tetrahedral coordination status and no extra-framework aluminum species .
Fig. S2 shows EDX results of samples 1, 2, 8 and 9. The Si/Al ratios calculated by 29Si MAS NMR have been shown in Table S3. For comparison, the Si/Al ratios from ICP-MS and EDX analyses are also presented in this table. As shown, the Si/Al ratios of the samples obtained from different analytical method are similar. The Si/Al ratios of these four samples are all less than 2, which are lower than that of the parent zeolite T (Si/Al ratio of about 3 ∼ 4). It is speculated that the parent zeolite is in a highly alkaline environment during the conversion process, which causing an increase in the silicon concentration in the liquid phase and the failure of some silica sol to integrate into the newly formed zeolite framework.
The acidity of these four samples can be finely analyzed by the pyridine- chemisorbed IR measurements and their results are shown in the Fig. S3. The detailed acid amounts of B acid and L acid at 150, 300 and 400 ◦C are listed in Table S5. According to previous reports , , the band at about 1440 cm−1 is attributed to chemisorbed pyridine on the Lewis acid sites, the band at about 1490 cm−1 is assigned to the pyridine bonded on the both Lewis and Brønsted acid site, the band at 1540 cm−1 is attributed to the Brønsted acid sites. All these four samples have much more Lewis acid sites than Brønsted acid sites. The samples treated by NaOH (samples 8 and 9) have higher Lewis and Brønsted acid sites compared to the samples treated by KOH (samples 1 and 2). All the samples have very weak Brønsted acid sites and their Lewis acid sites remain dominant position when the temperature increases to 400 °C. Regardless of the temperature, the acid amount of sample 2 is higher than that of sample 1 and the acid amount of sample 9 is higher than that of sample 8, which implies that the ultrasonic treatment can increase the amount of acid sites.
## The N2 and CO2 adsorption of samples
Fig. 9a shows N2 adsorption–desorption isotherms of samples 1, 2, 8 and 9. Samples 2 and 9 with the ultrasonic treatment exhibited more steep increases in adsorption values at high relative pressures than samples 1 and 8 without the ultrasonic treatment, which implies the generation of mesopores from particle agglomeration has been accelerate by dissolution of zeolite structure in sonochemical process. During sonication, formation of cavitation and the collapsing of bubbles produced huge energy which is sufficient for recrystallization of zeolite compared to the conventional hydrothermal treatment. Fig. 9b displays pore size distributions of the above samples, and samples 8 and 9 display relatively wider size distribution range of 3 ∼ 8 nm compared to samples 1 and 2 of about 3 ∼ 5 nm. The BET surface areas and pore volumes of samples 1, 2, 8 and 9 have been shown in Table S4. Fig. 9c and d present CO2 adsorption isotherms of the above four samples at 273 K (c) and 298 K (d). The pressure and temperature are factors that can influence CO2 adsorption. The CO2 adsorption capacity enhances with the increase in partial pressure of CO2 and all the samples show higher CO2 adsorption abilities at 273 K than 298 K. The adsorption capacity declined with temperature increasing for all the samples, which is in keeping with the other reports , , . At the constant temperature, the order of their CO2 adsorption abilities is sample 9 > sample 8 > sample 2 > sample 1, which is consistent with the order of pore volume of four samples. The samples 8 and 9 prepared in NaOH-treated solution have higher CO2 adsorption abilities than the samples 1 and 2 prepared in KOH-treated solution. In the same alkali solution, the sample with the ultrasonic treatment (sample 2 or 9) displays higher CO2 adsorption ability compared to the sample without the ultrasonic treatment (sample 1 or 8).
The CO2 adsorption affinity is influenced by the Si/Al ratio and low-silica zeolites show high CO2 adsorption capacities, because the negatively charged framework oxygens generated by the Al introduction enable the strong adsorption of acidic molecules such as CO2 , . As shown in Table S3, Si/Al ratios of samples in NaOH-treated solution determined by EDX and ICP results are a little lower than Si/Al ratios of samples in KOH-treated solution. The Si/Al ratios of four samples obtained by 29Si NMR results are very similar and their values are all less than about 2. It can be concluded that the zeolite CHA samples prepared in our experiment belongs to the category of low Si/Al ratio and hence possesses higher basicity, leading to higher CO2 capture.
The increase in surface energy resulting from the decrease in particle size can further enhance the interaction between the surface and CO2 , , which is beneficial for samples with the ultrasonic treatment exhibit higher CO2 adsorption ability compared to samples without the ultrasonic treatment. Creating secondary mesoporosity in zeolites is greatly helpful to accelerate the diffusion and mass transport of adsorbates to and away from the adsorption sites, because it can reduce grain boundaries and interfacial diffusion barriers . For all these four samples, the values of mesopore volumes are much higher than those of micropore volumes. Large mesopores can facilitate mass transfer and facile diffusion of gas molecules. The samples with higher mesopore volumes possess more excellent CO2 adsorption ability and the order of mesopore volumes is consistent with the order of CO2 uptakes. Sample 9 owns the largest mesopore volume of 0.145 cm3/g compared to other three samples (Table S4). Therefore, sample 9 exhibits the highest CO2 adsorption ability among the four samples, which is near to the value of nanosized Na-CHA prepared by Debost et al. . Na-CHA owned higher CO2 adsorption ability than K-CHA in their report, and the product synthesized from NaOH-treated solution has the higher CO2 adsorption ability than the sample in KOH-treated solution. Sample 9 produced in our experiment displays higher CO2 adsorption value than that of the micro-sized CHA crystals converted by FAU zeolite through solvent/OSDA-free route .
The nature of counter cation or exchangeable cation present in the zeolite framework cavities can strongly influence CO2 adsorption capacity, which is responsible for the basicity and electric field of zeolites . Only cations residing at the center of 8MRs can act as door-keepers to selectively admit guest molecules. Cations of different sizes and valences exhibit different site preferences and interaction abilities with the local environment . Na+ has higher interaction energy of CO2 with the zeolite cluster models than K+ . Compared to the Na+ ion (cation radius: 1.16 Å), the K+ ions with cation radius of 1.33 Å are preferred to reside at the center of 8MRs of CHA structure and can more effectively serve as the door-keeping cations , . The larger cations experiencing stronger interaction with the local environment of the 8MRs can lead to higher energy barriers ΔE (steeper potential wells) . Therefore, the samples 1 and 2 treated by KOH display lower adsorption capacities compared to samples 8 and 9 treated by NaOH.
## The evolution mechanism of zeolite transformation
Interzeolite conversion can give insight into fundamental zeolite formation mechanism besides providing an alternative synthesis route. Phase transformation has been evaluated as a function of time to further investigate on the mechanism of framework reassembly. Taking the condition of NaOH-treated solution and the ultrasonic temperature of 313 K as the study object, the XRD results of samples from 14 to 21 with different synthesis condition (the detailed information has been shown in Table 1) have been shown in Fig. S4. The sample 14 made by synthetic time of 6 h at 353 K without the ultrasonic treatment is zeolite T with lower characteristic peak compared to the original zeolite T because the crystal structure is getting collapse in the alkali solution. The sample 15 with ultrasonic treatment of 1 h at 313 K is still zeolite T with the similar characteristic peaks of sample 14. The sample 16 prepared by ultrasonic time of 2 h at 313 K displays more indistinct zeolite T peaks compared to sample 14, which indicates that the synergistic effect of the strong power with ultrasound facilitates the collapse of zeolite T structure. With the continuous alkaline effect at synthetic temperature of 353 K, sample 17 prepared by 6 h shows very weak zeolite T peaks and sample 18 prepared by 12 h exhibits more amorphous form. And then, the CHA phase peaks have been started to grow with increasing the synthetic time on the XRD observations of sample 19 (synthetic time of 20 h) and sample 20 (synthetic time of 28 h). Finally, as shown in XRD pattern of sample 21, the pure CHA phase with more distinct characteristic peaks has been formed with the synthetic time of 40 h.
FT-IR spectroscopy has also been applied to track the structural evolution of these samples and the results have been shown in Fig. S5. Sample 14 shows the most characteristic bands of zeolite T topology , after 6 h at 353 K in NaOH solution without the ultrasonic treatment. The absorption bands in the range of 575 to 615 cm−1 are attributed to double-6 rings (D6R) (The gray area in Fig. S5) of zeolite T structure , . The peaks of sample 15 with the ultrasonic treatment of 1 h are very similar to those of sample 14. For sample 16 with the ultrasonic treatment of 2 h, the characteristic peaks of zeolite T are much weaker than that of sample 14, which indicates that the ultrasound can effectively promote the collapse of zeolite T structure. It can be seen that sample 18 has the very subtle D6R vibration range compared to sample 17. Sample 19 with the synthetic time of 20 h has the characteristic vibration of double-6 rings (D6R) at 640 cm−1 for zeolite CHA . With increasing the recrystallization time to 28 h, the characteristic bands of zeolite CHA structure are getting more clearly for sample 20. Finally, after 40 h of recrystallization, sample 21 has the successful reconstruction of CHA phase. The results of XRD and FTIR data have initially suggested that there is the dissolution of the starting zeolite and the restructuration of the species to form the target structure.
In order to observe the morphological changes during the process of interzeolite transformation, SEM analysis has been carried out and Fig. S6 has depicted the detailed relevant progress. It is clear that sample 14 (Fig. S6a) displays looser nanoparticle aggregates with irregular shape compared to that of the original sample 1(Fig. 1a). Fig. S6b shows the morphology of sample 16, which owns more amorphous gel substances compared to sample 14. The SEM image of sample 17 in Fig. S6c presents more obvious trend of amorphous form. Further increasing the synthetic time, the primitive disk-like shape of CHA phase has been detected on the SEM image of sample 19 (Fig. S6d). And the original walnut-like zeolite CHA crystals have been taken shape after the reactive time of 28 h, which is marked with circle in Fig. S6e of sample 20. As shown in Fig. S6f, CHA crystals with more regular shape have been formed for sample 21 with the synthetic time of 40 h at 353 K. In the reference of Geng et al. , the dissolution of zeolite A and the generation of SSZ-13 zeolite are simultaneous at lower temperature of 130 ℃, while zeolite A has almost completely dissolved and then SSZ-13 has crystallized quickly at higher temperature of 150℃. The observation in our experiment is similar with the condition of lower temperature in this report. Furthermore, the SEM result in this paper is also consistent with the SEM analysis in the report of Kim et al. , they found that the dissolved zeolite Y crystals were assembled to form a secondary amorphous phase, on which the aggregated particles of CHA were crystallized.
The schematic diagram displayed in Fig. 10 visually describes the transformation of zeolite T to zeolite CHA. Because the main phase (OFF) has the similar structure with the minor phase (ERI) for zeolite T, OFF has been chosen as the parent phase in this figure. The building units of OFF phase and CHA phase are from the CIF file of Materials Studio 6.0. The evolutionary process of products can be summarized in three stages. The first stage is the generation of locally ordered aluminosilicate species via the dissolution of the starting zeolite T. And then, the secondary amorphous phase leads to the nucleation of CHA at the convenience of the structural similarity (i.e. D6R), which can reduce the kinetic barrier for the nucleation of CHA phase. Consequently, CHA crystals with the well-defined structure have regrown under the adequate condition at the final step. Observed by the whole process, the critical point is that the parent zeolite decomposes into secondary structural units that can be converted into another zeolite under reasonable conditions. The structural similarity between the original zeolite and the final zeolite can be considered the important factor of successful interconversion of two zeolites . The ultrasonic treatment on the first stage is beneficial to the effective dissolution of zeolite T structure in short time and can make more small nuclei and decrease the crystals size of zeolite CHA.
Predicting the conditions for synthesis and relating them to the structures obtained is a challenge, especially for metastable materials . In our experiment, the less dense CHA phase with lower framework density (FDSi = 15.1 T/1000 Å3) has been converted from the more stable zeolite T nanocrystal aggregates with higher framework density (FDSi = 16.1 T/1000 Å3). This result confirms the possibility of transforming zeolite into less dense zeolites and the synthesis condition should be carefully controlled. The appropriate ultrasound temperature is 313 K, while the higher ultrasound temperature (333 K) is easy to form the by-product of MER phase or GIS phase with higher framework density (FDSi = 16.4 T/1000 Å3). Li et al. have prepared a high-quality MER-rich product from coal fly ashes by KOH direct conversion method under optimal activation conditions. Mokgehle et al. have obtained GIS product from the ultrasonication of coal fly ash/NaOH slurry filtrates. It seems that MER phase is easy to be formed in the KOH-rich condition and GIS phase is inclined to the produced in the NaOH-rich condition from the same original materials. Literature reported that the formation of D6R units could be promoted by Na+, and the result in this paper somewhat reinforced this statement. The mixed-crystal phenomenon in NaOH-treated solution is weaker than that in KOH-treated solution. The formation and evolution of D6R units act as an essential role in determining the crystallization efficiency and the size of crystals . The size of CHA crystals recrystallized from NaOH-treated solution is smaller than that from KOH-treated solution, and CO2 adsorption ability of the former is better than that of the latter.
By far, the interzeolite conversion is the complicated process and the perception is still primary and indistinct. As the recent review of Zhang et al. pointed, to have a clear understanding at the molecular level on the transformed synthesis of zeolite, it is needed to develop the in situ/ex situ characterization techniques with atomic resolution for monitoring the crystallization process and the multi-scale simulations for nucleation/growth process of zeolites. In this short review, they briefly summarized the heterologous seed-assisted syntheses of zeolites and analyzed the structure-directing effect of heterologous seeds. The “common composite building units (CBUs) hypothesis” and the “common secondary building units (SBUs) hypothesis” on the explanation of interzeolite conversion have been surveyed in this paper. The transformation of zeolite T to zeolite CHA in this paper can be explained by this kind of statement. The synthetic systems that there is no common CBU between the seed zeolite and the target zeolite also have been listed in this paper, such as the case of zeolite CHA to zeolite MER. They proposed that the formation of the nuclei of the target zeolite can be promoted the facilitated appropriate distribution of particular “species”, “fragments”, or “building units”. In our experiment, the final zeolite CHA has the tendency to MER phase or GIS phase with more stable structure in some certain synthetic condition. Is there a possibility that pure MER phase or GIS phase can be converted by zeolite T through the intermediary CHA phase through intensively adjusting the synthetic process? The correlative exploration is undergoing based on this thought and this research work is helpful for the further understanding on the interzeolite mechanism.
## Conclusion
Using zeolite T as the parent zeolite precursor, ultrasonic-assisted interzeolite transformation offers an efficient route to the preparation of the pure CHA phase. The products with and without the ultrasonic pretreatment have been contrasted in both KOH solution and NaOH solution. The synthesis conditions have been carefully investigated, because CHA phase is metastable and the products is easy to mixed with more stable MER or GIS phase. The samples with ultrasonic treatment have displayed better CO2 adsorption abilities than samples without the ultrasonic treatment because of larger mesopore volume. The interzeolite process can be concluded into three stages on the research of transform mechanism: the dissolution of zeolite T, the intermediate phase and the recrystallization of zeolite CHA. The ultrasonic treatment at the first step can effectively accelerate the collapse of zeolite T framework and make more nuclei, which is favorable to obtain smaller crystal size and higher CO2 adsorption capacity.
