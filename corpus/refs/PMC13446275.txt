## Introduction
Green hydrogen is gaining attention as a clean and sustainable fuel that can play a crucial role in addressing global and environmental challenges as it produces hydrogen by water splitting using renewable energy. It has the potential to reduce reliance on fossil fuels in the chemical, steel, and transportation sectors while supporting long-term energy sustainability, which makes green hydrogen a cornerstone of future clean energy strategies.[[cit1]][[cit2]] Photocatalytic water splitting is a promising method for green hydrogen production which uses solar energy, the most abundant clean renewable source, to convert water into hydrogen and oxygen, making it a carbon-neutral approach for hydrogen production unlike conventional hydrogen production that relies on fossil fuels and electricity-intensive electrolysis.[[cit2]][[cit3]] Various photocatalysts with visible-light band gaps below 3 eV (e.g., metal oxides, sulfides, and oxynitrides) have been studied to maximize solar absorption, given that visible light comprises 45% of the solar spectrum, compared to only 3% for UV light.[[cit4]]
Cadmium sulphide (CdS) is one of the most promising catalysts as it has a narrow bandgap (∼2.4 eV) and has shown good photocatalytic water splitting under visible light.[[cit5]][[cit6]][[cit7]] The narrow bandgap enables the catalyst to absorb a significant portion of the solar spectrum, leading to higher catalytic activity than catalysts with a wide bandgap like TiO2. The conduction band (CB) level of CdS is sufficiently negative relative to the H+ reduction potential, i.e., the photogenerated e− is efficiently reduced from H+ to H2, making it effective for driving the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER).[[cit8]] CdS can be synthesized by one-pot or hydrothermal methods without the use of complex precursors or harsh conditions, making it easy to scale up. Bare CdS shows good photocatalytic activity, but its conversion efficiency is limited because of the fast recombination of the photogenerated charge carriers and CdS, and it can undergo photocorrosion when photoexcited h+ oxidize the S2− anions, leading to degradation of the catalyst. The photocorrosion issue can be tackled by using sacrificial agents, especially Na2S/Na2SO3, which create a sulphur-rich environment that acts as a sink for h+, preventing the oxidation of S2−.[[cit9]] The excitons (e− and h+ pair) recombination of co-catalysts can be prevented by forming heterojunctions and by engineering various morphologies.[[cit10]][[cit11]][[cit12]][[cit13]]
The performance of CdS is dependent on the morphology as it affects the surface area, charge separation efficiency, and resistance to photocorrosion.[[cit14]] Nanorods and nanoneedles have shown superior catalytic activity as compared to nanowires and nanobelts due to their lower electron–hole recombination rates and reduced number of defect centres. As the dimensionality of CdS decreases, the VB edges stabilize, which improves the photostability.[[cit15]] Bo Zhang et al. studied the effect of the morphology of CdS on the hydrogen evolution reaction and found that branched/granular CdS showed better activity than agglomerated cauliflower-like CdS, as the branched CdS had greater exposure of the active sites and more charge-efficient paths.[[cit16]] In the work done by YuLin Min et al., branched CdS/MoS2 had a higher density of interfaces between CdS and MoS2 sheets, which increased the interfacial contact and boosted the electron–hole separation efficiency. Thus, branched CdS/MoS2 showed better catalytic activity than pure CdS and unbranched morphologies.[[cit17]]
To further enhance the activity, research on noble-metal deposition on CdS has been done to form a metal–semiconductor heterojunction. Heterojunctions facilitate efficient electron transfer from CdS to metal sites.[[cit18]][[cit19]][[cit20]][[cit21]] The metallic characteristics of noble metals, with respect to work functions, allow photogenerated electrons in the CB of CdS to transfer easily to the noble-metal sites. This electron-sink behaviour reduces charge recombination and increases the availability of electrons for H+ reduction to H2 at the metal site. Noble metals also provide additional active sites for hydrogen formation once the electrons reach the metal.[[cit22]][[cit23]][[cit24]][[cit25]] Incorporating Pd as a co-catalyst has emerged as an effective strategy to substantially elevate the catalytic activity of pristine catalysts. Pd/TiO2 nanocubes showed an increase in photocatalytic activity because Pd acts as an electron sink, which inhibits the electrons from recombining with the holes. The cubic morphology of Pd helped in directional charge separation and efficient transfer of electrons at the Pd catalytic sites.[[cit26]] Incorporation of Pd in the homopolymer matrix of P10 (dibenzo[b,d]thiophene sulphone) facilitated the efficient capture of excited electrons.[[cit27]] An alloy of Pd with Ni (PdNi) was synthesised, and these hollow spherical nanoparticles were anchored on the CdS catalyst to enhance the photocatalytic performance. In this system, PdNi prevented excessive electron accumulation, thereby enhancing the stability of the catalyst.[[cit28]]
Branched morphologies facilitate reduced electron–hole recombination due to shorter and multiple pathways for charge-carrier migration to the surface. It is expected that the use of various precursors and structural directing agents will lead to different CdS morphologies, which will affect the photocatalytic activity during water splitting. Impregnation of Pd is expected to increase the activity due to the formation of metal–semiconductor junctions between CdS and Pd. This is because Schottky barriers are formed at the interface that traps the e− in Pd, preventing the recombination of charge carriers. Additionally, increasing the Pd loading is expected to give an optimum loading at which the hydrogen production is maximum, after which the activity will decrease as excessive loading may obstruct light absorption or introduce recombination pathways.
In this study, CdS catalysts with varied morphologies were synthesized by the hydrothermal method, using different precursors and structural directing agents, to study the influence of morphologies on the photocatalytic activity for water splitting under simulated solar irradiation. Furthermore, palladium (Pd) was impregnated onto the CdS surface to form metal–semiconductor junctions starting from 1 wt% Pd loading and increasing to 5 wt% to study the effect of increased metal loading. The structural properties were characterized by X-ray diffraction (XRD), morphology was observed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM), optical properties were studied by UV-visible diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (UV-DRS), for bandgap determination, photoluminescence spectroscopy (PL) was used to study the recombination of charge carriers, Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) analysis was used to determine the surface area, and the Pd dispersion on CdS was studied by CO pulse chemisorption. Thus, this study will help to understand the effects of morphology, the formation of metal–semiconductor junctions with Pd, and the effects of increased Pd loading on the photocatalytic activity for water splitting.
## Experiments and methods
## Chemicals
Cadmium chloride (dried anhydrous; CdCl2; ACS, 99%), sodium sulphide flakes (Na2S, 60%), polyethylene glycol with molecular weight 3350 and 9000 (PEG 3350 and PEG 9000), ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), hydrazine hydrate (N2H4·H2O, extrapure, 80%), and sodium sulphite (Na2SO3, 98%) were purchased from Sisco Research Laboratories Pvt. Ltd, India. Thiourea (CH4N2S, 98%) was obtained from Avra, India; hydrochloric acid (HCl) was from Qualigens, India; and palladium chloride (PdCl2, 99.9%) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, U.S.
## Synthesis of CdS with different morphologies
Table 1 shows the CdS catalysts with various morphologies synthesized by the hydrothermal method. CdCl2 was used as the cadmium precursor, and thiourea and Na2S were used as sulphur precursors. Separate aqueous solutions of cadmium and sulphur precursors were prepared in a molar ratio of 1 : 3, and templates such as PEG 3350, PEG 9000, and EDTA were also added. The solutions were mixed and added to a Teflon-lined stainless steel autoclave and heated at 200 °C for 4 h in a hot-air oven. After that, the products were then filtered, washed with distilled water and ethanol, and dried in a vacuum oven at 60 °C for 4 h to obtain CdS.

## Chemical reactions
Reaction with thiourea: CdCl2 + CS(NH2)2 + H2O → CdS ↓ + 2NH+4 + 2Cl− + CO2
Reaction with sodium sulphide: CdCl2 + Na2S + H2O → CdS ↓ + 2NaCl.
## Wet impregnation procedure for Pd deposition onto CdS
Pd was deposited on the surface of CdS by the wet impregnation method. The prepared CdS was dispersed in distilled water and magnetically stirred for efficient dispersion for one hour. A PdCl2 aqueous solution was prepared, and HCl was added to it to render the PdCl2 soluble in distilled water. The PdCl2 solution was then added to CdS dispersed in water and stirred for one hour. Hydrazine hydrate was added to reduce Pd2+ to Pd0, and the mixture was stirred for one hour. It was then filtered, washed with distilled water, dried at 60 °C for 4 h, and then calcined at 200 °C for 4 h. Pd was impregnated on the CdS surface from 1 wt% to 5 wt%.
## Characterization techniques
To characterize the crystalline structure of the synthesized cadmium sulphide (CdS) catalysts, X-ray diffraction analysis was performed with a Rigaku Ultima IV X-ray diffractometer (XRD) with Cu Kα radiation, with a scanning rate of 1°/min and a step size of 0.01 from 2θ angles of 20° to 60°. Morphological analysis was performed to observe the shape of CdS. The catalyst was dispersed in acetone and then drop-casted on a silicon wafer and gold-sputtered with a Leica Ultramicrotome (EM UC7, Germany). Field-emission scanning electron spectroscopy (FESEM) was performed using an FEI instrument (Apreo LoVac, USA), with a Retractable STEM 3+ Detector used to obtain images. Elemental mapping of Pd was performed with an Aztec Standard EDS system. A Jasco UV-VIS-NIR spectrophotometer (UV-670, Japan) was used to perform diffuse reflectance spectroscopy studies to determine the bandgap of the catalysts. A Jasco Spectrofluorometer (FP-6300, Japan) was used to determine the recombination of photogenerated charge carriers. High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) was performed with a Jem-2100P instrument (JEOL, Japan) operating under high vacuum, with a magnification of up to 3 000 00×. The photocatalysis studies were performed using a solar simulator (Sciencetech Inc, Sci Sun 300, UK) with a Xe lamp and 1.5 AM filter as light source, and a gas chromatograph (GC) with thermal conductivity detection (TCD) (Mayura Analytical Ltd, India) was used to quantify the hydrogen gas produced. To evaluate the metal dispersion at loadings of 1–5 wt%, CO pulse chemisorption experiments were conducted using a TCD detector. This technique was used to assess the number of surface palladium sites available for chemisorption and to calculate the metal dispersion on the surface of the catalyst. About 100 mg of catalyst was placed in a U-shaped quartz reactor and pretreated under helium at 100 °C for 30 min to remove physically adsorbed species. Subsequently, a 5% CO/He mixture was passed at 30 mL min−1, where CO selectively adsorbed on Pd surface sites. Pulses of CO were introduced until saturation, and the amount adsorbed was measured and calibrated to determine palladium dispersion.
## Experimental setup and operating conditions
Photocatalytic experiments were conducted in a quartz reactor containing 200 mL of distilled water, and 0.6 mM aqueous solutions of Na2S and Na2SO3 of 50 mL each were added as sacrificial agents. The experiment was carried out using 50 mg of catalyst, while a control experiment was also performed using only the sacrificial agents. The suspension was kept under continuous magnetic stirring for uniform dispersion of the catalyst. Argon gas was purged through the reactor at a flow rate of 50 mL min−1 for 20 min to remove dissolved gases, then 20 mL min−1 for the rest of the experiment. A solar simulator (Sciencetech Inc., Sci Sun 300, UK) with a 1.5 AM filter was used as the source of light, and the evolved gases were analysed using gas chromatography at 1 h intervals for 12 h.[[cit29]] Control studies were also conducted to study the effect of the sacrificial agents, and the photocatalytic activity analysis is shown in Fig. S1 of the SI (Section S1).
## Results and discussion
## Structural and phase analysis using XRD
The crystalline structure of the synthesised CdS was investigated based on the X-ray diffraction patterns, as shown in Fig. 1(a). It was found that sulphur precursors had an effect on the crystal phase formed. In samples CS_PEG3350, CS_PEG9000 and CS_EDTA, when thiourea was used as sulphur precursor, the XRD pattern showed characteristic peaks (2θ: 24.8°, 26.5°, 28.2°, 36.6°, 43.7°, 47.9°, 50.9°, 51.9°, and 52.8°) of the hexagonal (wurtzite) phase of CdS, as confirmed by JCPDS no. 01-077-2306. In contrast, CdS_Na2S, where Na2S was used as a sulphur precursor, showed a different XRD pattern that matched JCPDS no. 01-075-1546 (2θ: 26.5°, 43.9°, and 51.9°), which corresponds to the cubic (zinc blende) phase of CdS. The crystal phase of CdS exhibited a mixed phase of hexagonal and cubic, with cubic being the predominant phase. This observation helped identify the dependency of the crystal phase of CdS on the sulphur precursor.[[cit30]]
XRD analysis of the Pd-impregnated CdS samples (Fig. 1(b)) was carried out to evaluate whether the incorporation of Pd had any influence on the crystalline structure of bare CdS. The diffraction patterns obtained for the Pd-loaded samples showed similar patterns, with all the characteristic peaks corresponding to CdS present and well aligned. Also, no additional diffraction peaks or noticeable peak shifts were observed upon Pd impregnation, indicating that the introduction of Pd did not alter the intrinsic crystal structure of CdS. This suggests that Pd exists in a highly dispersed form on the CdS surface rather than forming any separate crystalline Pd or Pd-related phases.[[cit31]][[cit32]]
## Morphological insights from SEM, TEM analysis and N2 adsorption–desorption analysis
SEM and TEM analysis of the synthesized CdS reveals that the choice of precursors and templates significantly affects its morphology. For Na2S-derived CdS, as shown in Fig. 2(a), the SEM analysis of the products revealed uniformly distributed spherical nanoparticles of average particle sizes 19 ± 3 nm. On the other hand, for thiourea (CS(NH2)2) as a sulphur source, the material shows a completely different morphology. For CS_PEG3350, CS_PEG9000 and CS_EDTA, hierarchical morphology was observed, as shown in Fig. 2(b–d), in which nanosized particles are self-assembled as larger micron-sized particles in various shapes, such as spherical, leaf-like and flower-like, for CdS prepared with EDTA, PEG 3350 and PEG 9000, respectively.
When thiourea is used as a sulphur precursor, the Cd-thiourea complex is formed in the solution. At elevated temperatures, Cd2+ and S2− ions are released gradually due to the breaking of the complex. This slow and controlled release of ions elongated the growth of particles, leading to the self-assembly of hierarchical shapes such as leaf-like, flower-like, and spherical microparticles. But when Na2S is used as a sulphur precursor, a faster release of S2− ions occurs because Na2S is an ionic molecule that dissociates completely in aqueous solution, causing faster nucleation and growth of CdS particles to the nanosize range. The slow release of sulphide ions favours directed growth through oriented attachment and hierarchical structure development, favouring elongated and complicated shapes at the expense of simpler spherical nanoparticles. This contrast between sulphide-ion release rates directly influences both the nucleation energy barrier and the following particle growth mode, giving a distinct difference in particle shape and particle number distributions between Na2S and thiourea-derived samples of CdS particles.[[cit33]]
BET surface area analysis was carried out by N2 adsorption–desorption to investigate the influence of morphology on the surface area of CdS samples with different structural features. The results revealed a clear dependence of surface area on morphology. Among the samples, CS_Na2S exhibited the highest specific surface area of 55.6 m2 g−1, which can be attributed to its nanometre-scale particle size. In contrast, CdS samples with hierarchical morphologies, CS_EDTA, CS_PEG9000 and CS_PEG3350, showed lower surface areas of 4.9, 1.3 and 1.2 m2 g−1, respectively. This may be due to particle aggregation and the compact nature of the assembled structures, which reduce the accessible surface area.
## Bandgap determination by UV-DRS
Bandgap energies of the CdS photocatalysts were analysed by UV-DRS, which was used to determine their light-absorption characteristics and potential as visible-light-driven photocatalysts. The optical bandgap energy was calculated by converting the measured reflectance data into the Kubelka–Munk (K–M) function, F(R), proportional to the absorption coefficient (α). Fig. 3 shows a Tauc plot plotted with [F(R)·hν]n against photon energy (hν) of a semiconductor, where n is representative of a semiconductor's electronic transition type. From analysis of Tauc plots, all of the bare CdS samples showed a bandgap energy falling into the visible-light portion of the electromagnetic spectrum, i.e., 2.20, 2.37, 2.35 and 2.07 eV for CS_Na2S, CS_PEG3350, CS_PEG9000 and CS_EDTA, respectively. This bandgap value enables the CdS photocatalysts to absorb visible light effectively and employ it for photocatalytic reactions like hydrogen production. The small bandgap also reveals effective light-harvesting capability, so CdS holds potential as a visible-light-active photocatalyst for photovoltaic applications powered by sunlight. For Pd-impregnated CdS, it was observed that the bandgap of all the materials decreased; i.e., 1.75, 2.34, 2.30 and 1.99 eV for CS_Na2S, CS_PEG3350, CS_PEG9000 and CS_EDTA, respectively. This shows that the formation of a metal–semiconductor junction decreases the bandgap of the catalyst, which can help to improve the photocatalytic activity. CS_Na2S showed the lowest bandgap after formation of a metal–semiconductor junction with 1 wt% Pd deposition on CdS. The bandgap decreased even more after increasing the Pd deposition from 1.75 eV for 1 wt% to 1.43 eV for 5 wt%, as summarized in Table 2.

## Recombination of charge carriers
PL was performed to analyse the recombination behaviour of photogenerated electron–hole pairs. From Fig. 4, it can be seen that PL intensities varied among different CdS morphologies, indicating their distinct charge-carrier dynamics. CS_Na2S exhibited the lowest PL intensity, indicating better charge separation, which can be due to its smallest particle size among all the materials. In comparison, CS_PEG3350, CS_PEG9000 and CS_EDTA showed higher PL intensities, corresponding to higher recombination rates. This is because smaller particles allow electrons to reach the surface more easily, resulting in better charge separation and reduced recombination. In contrast, larger particles have charge carriers that recombine before reaching the surface, preventing them from participating in redox reactions and producing hydrogen and oxygen.[[cit34]][[cit35]] These differences in PL intensities show how morphology can influence defect states and charge-carrier pathways in CdS, which can further affect the photocatalytic performance.
PL analysis was also performed for the Pd-impregnated CdS sample to evaluate the impact of metal deposition on charge-carrier separation. The Pd deposition on CdS leads to the formation of metal–semiconductor junctions, which facilitates efficient charge separation by enabling the transfer of photogenerated electrons from the CB of CdS to Pd.[[cit36]][[cit37]][[cit38]] This suppresses electron–hole recombination, which can be observed in the reduced PL intensities of these samples compared to that of CdS. Similar trends of intensities were observed for the Pd-impregnated samples; i.e., PCS_Na2S showed the lowest intensity while PCS_PEG3300, PCS_PEG9000 and PCS_EDTA showed higher intensities.
## Catalytic activity for hydrogen generation
## Photocatalytic performance of CdS with various morphologies
Photocatalytic hydrogen evolution reactions were conducted to examine the activity of CdS samples with varying morphologies, prepared using different sulphur precursors and surfactants. The activities showed a strong dependence of photocatalytic activity on the particle size and morphology of the CdS photocatalysts, as shown in Fig. 5. Of all the samples investigated, CS_Na2S, comprised of spherical nanoparticles, exhibited the highest hydrogen evolution rate of 1009 µmol g−1 under visible-light illumination. The highest activity of this sample is primarily attributed to its smallest particle size of 19 ± 3 nm, which yields a high specific surface area and a large number of active sites that are accessible for photocatalytic reactions. Additionally, its spherical shape promotes easy charge separation and migration, resulting in a reduction of electron–hole recombination loss.
The leaf-like and flower-like CS_PEG3350 and CS_PEG9000 morphologies, respectively, showed lower hydrogen production rates of 896.6 µmol g−1 and 785.7 µmol g−1, respectively. The samples have branched, hierarchical structures, which also favour absorption of light and exposure of active sites, resulting in their comparatively high photocatalysis activity. However, due to their larger particle sizes, compared to CS_Na2S, the longer charge-carrier diffusion routes result in a moderate loss of activity.
The self-assembled micro-spherical CS_EDTA exhibited the lowest hydrogen evolution activity of 598.9 µmol g−1. Such relatively weak activity can be attributed to its larger aggregated spherical morphology and smaller surface area, which limits the availability of surface-active sites. Additionally, due to the larger particle size, it is difficult for charge carriers to reach the surface of the particles without recombining, leading to reduced hydrogen generation efficiency. The overall trend of photocatalyst activity follows the order CS_Na2S (spherical) > CS_PEG3350 (leaf-like) > CS_PEG9000 (flower-like) > CS_EDTA (micro-spherical). This clearly demonstrates that surface structure, morphology, and particle size have a significant influence on the efficiency of photocatalysts based on CdS compounds. The spherical, well-dispersed, smaller-sized nanoparticles have increased surface reactivity and charge utilization, thereby exhibiting the highest hydrogen evolution rate.
## Effect of Pd deposition (metal–semiconductor (M–S) heterojunction)
To increase the photocatalytic efficiency of CdS, 1 wt% of Pd was deposited onto the surface of the CdS photocatalysts to create an M–S junction. The addition of Pd introduces Schottky junctions at the metal–semiconductor interface, which are crucial for enhancing charge separation and transfer during the photocatalytic reaction. Under light irradiation, photoexcited electrons produced in the conduction band of CdS travel towards the nanoparticle sites of Pd based on a positive work function difference between CdS and Pd. The sites of Pd serve as electron sinks (co-catalysts), where electrons are trapped and cannot recombine with holes of the CdS valence band. Efficient charge-carrier separation results in a higher number of electrons for proton reduction, thereby improving the hydrogen evolution reaction rate.
All of the Pd-loaded CdS samples exhibited remarkably enhanced photocatalytic performance compared to the corresponding bare CdS, as shown in Fig. 6(a), affirming the positive effect of Pd for boosting interfacial charge kinetics. Of all the samples investigated, PCS_Na2S showed a high hydrogen production rate of 1345.7 µmol.g−1, which is due to its small, spherical, uniformly shaped nanoparticles and Pd dispersion.
PCS_PEG3350 and PCS_PEG9000, which exhibited leaf-like and flower-like morphologies, also exhibited improved activities of 1081.9 µmol g−1 and 896.6 µmol g−1, respectively. Such branched and hierarchical morphologies enable multiple active sites and beneficial light scattering, resulting in enhanced photon absorption and improved catalysis efficiency. However, partial aggregation leading to the formation of larger CdS particles results in reduced photocatalytic performance compared to that of the PCS_Na2S catalyst.
The sample of PCS_EDTA showed the lowest hydrogen evolution rate (742.9 µmol g−1) of all the Pd-loaded photocatalysts. The relatively low activity can be attributed to its crowded, self-assembled micro-spherical structure, which diminishes the readily available surface area and inhibits facile charge transfer between CdS and Pd. Furthermore, excessive particle aggregation tends to occur, giving rise to localized charge recombination sites, thereby lowering the efficiency of the photocatalytic reactions. The overall activity trend for the Pd-loaded catalyst was PCS_Na2S > PCS_PEG3350 > PCS_PEG9000 > PCS_EDTA, which is consistent with the trend seen for the bare CdS samples. As indicated in Fig. 6(b), the contrast in catalytic activity between bare CdS and Pd-impregnated CdS supports the conclusion that hydrogen evolution is greatly improved by Pd loading across all morphologies. Such improvement supports the synthesis of an effective metal–semiconductor junction in which the Pd co-catalyst promotes charge-carrier separation and facilitates surface reduction to accelerate hydrogen production.
## Effect of increased Pd deposition
Of the various morphologies prepared, CS_Na2S, which had a spherical nanoparticle structure, was found to exhibit the highest catalytic activity. This increased catalytic activity was observed for both cases: when the semiconductor catalyst was in its pristine form, as well as when a heterojunction was formed with the impregnation of Pd into the semiconductor catalyst. From these results, CS_Na2S was chosen for further study in order to determine how the concentration of metals influences its catalytic properties. In order to do this, the concentration of Pd in CS_Na2S was increased from 1 wt% to 5 wt%. This study was conducted to determine how an increased concentration improves the catalytic activity, especially in terms of how it enhances the active sites, charge transfer, and efficiency of the catalysis.
## Structural analysis using TEM and HRTEM
Fig. 7 shows the TEM images obtained for the most active sample, CS_Na2S, revealing detailed information about its structural and crystal properties. HRTEM images were used to obtain information about the crystal structure. The lattice fringes in the HRTEM image correspond to an interplanar spacing (d-spacing) of 0.326 nm for (111) plane, confirming the crystalline structure of the CS_Na2S sample; for 3 wt% PCS_Na2S, the d-spacing was 0.34 nm for (111) plane.
In addition, selected-area electron diffraction (SAED) studies were conducted to obtain information about the crystal structure. The presence of diffraction rings/spots corresponding to crystal planes such as (111), (220), (311), (331), and (422) indicates the formation of a crystalline structure in CS_Na2S. The presence of these crystal planes indicates the formation of a highly crystalline structure in CS_Na2S, which is expected to improve the catalytic performance by facilitating charge transport.

## Optical properties
In order to study the influence of increasing Pd loading on the optical properties of the catalyst, such as band gap energy and recombination of photo-generated charge carriers, UV-DRS and PL measurements were carried out, as shown in Fig. 9. From the results of the UV-DRS measurements, it was observed that there was a shift in the absorption band with increasing Pd loading, indicating a change in the electronic structure of the catalyst upon impregnation with metals. The band gap energy decreased from 1.75 eV to 1.43 eV with increasing Pd loading. This may be because Pd deposition introduces modifications to the electronic environment of the CdS semiconductor, creating localized states in the energy gap, causing sub-gap absorption and leading to a lower band-gap energy, as shown in the Tauc plot analysis. The reduction in band gap energy indicates that there is an increase in absorption of visible light by the semiconductor, which might improve its photocatalytic activity.[[cit39]][[cit40]]
In order to study the recombination of photogenerated electron–hole pairs, PL measurements were carried out. The PL intensity reduced with increasing Pd loading, indicating better charge-carrier separation due to the formation of a metal–semiconductor interface. Among all the samples prepared with varying loadings of Pd, the 3% Pd-loaded sample showed the lowest PL intensity, indicating efficient suppression of electron–hole pair recombination. Improved charge separation in the 3% Pd-loaded sample can be attributed to the uniform dispersion of Pd on the CdS surface, which facilitates the formation of an efficient metal–semiconductor junction. 3 wt% Pd–CdS_Na2S had highest quenching efficiency of 24%, as per the calculations shown in Section S3 of the SI. The highest quenching efficiency further confirms 3 wt% Pd–CdS_Na2S as the best catalyst.
## Charge-transfer characteristics from EIS analysis
The Nyquist plots shown in Fig. 10 reveal that the 3 wt% Pd/CdS catalyst exhibits the smallest semicircular diameter among all the samples, indicating the lowest charge-transfer resistance (Rct). The reduced Rct suggests more efficient migration of photogenerated electrons across the Pd/CdS interface. The formation of a Schottky junction between Pd and CdS promotes rapid electron extraction from the CdS conduction band, while Pd acts as an electron sink, suppressing electron–hole recombination. Consequently, more electrons participate in proton reduction, resulting in enhanced hydrogen evolution.[[cit41]] The EIS results are in excellent agreement with the PL measurements, which show the lowest emission intensity for the 3 wt% Pd/CdS catalyst, confirming efficient charge separation.
## Electronic band structure and edge alignment
The positions of the band edges in the semiconductor CdS and the metal–semiconductor junction in Pd–CdS were studied using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy valence band spectra in order to understand the effect of Pd loading on the catalytic properties. The VBM values were obtained from the valence band spectra shown in Fig. 11, and the CBM values were calculated using the values obtained from UV-DRS spectra. From the results, it was found that the VBM value in CdS and 1 wt% Pd/CdS is 1.05 eV, which indicates that even with a small percentage of Pd deposition on the semiconductor, the valence band position is not significantly altered. However, a significant change in the conduction band was observed. The CBM was found to shift from −1.15 eV in pristine CdS to −0.70 eV in 1 wt% Pd/CdS, which indicates a change in the conduction band. In further studies, when the Pd content was increased to 3 wt%, significant changes in both CB and VB were observed. The VBM was found at 0.52 eV, and the CB was found at −1.01 eV. These changes in CB and VB positions are expected to enhance the catalytic properties. These modifications in the conduction and valence bands are expected to enhance the catalytic properties.
Before the contact (Fig. 11(b)), there are different Fermi levels in CdS and Pd due to the difference in their work functions. There are higher Fermi levels in CdS, which is an n-type semiconductor, than in Pd because Pd has a higher work function. When contact is formed between CdS and Pd, electrons will move from CdS to Pd to achieve an equilibrium wherein the Fermi levels will be equal. This will result in positively charged ions of the donor atoms in CdS forming a depletion layer, along with the formation of upward band bending in the CdS/Pd junction. Along with this, the accumulation of excess electrons occurs on the Pd side of the junction, forming a Schottky barrier.
Upon illumination with visible light, the photon energy absorbed is equal to or higher than the bandgap of CdS. As a result, the formation of electron–hole pairs occurs due to the excitation of electrons from the valence band to the conduction band. In this case, the motion of the photogenerated electrons to the Pd nanoparticles occurs due to the presence of a built-in electric field and the Schottky barrier, which ensures efficient trapping. As a result, Pd acts as an electron sink and a catalytic reduction site for the hydrogen evolution reaction. The accumulated electrons reduce the adsorbed protons into hydrogen, while the photogenerated holes present in the valence band of the CdS take part in the oxidation process.[[cit42]]
## X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy
X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy was used to analyze the chemical composition and oxidation states of the elements Cd, S, and Pd on the surface, as shown in Fig. 12. For the Cd 3d spectrum, two distinct peaks were seen for bare CdS: the Cd 3d3/2 and Cd 3d5/2 peaks are located at 411.88 and 405.13 eV, respectively. After Pd deposition, these peaks shift to 411.78 and 405.10 eV, respectively. The observed shift in the Cd core-level binding energies indicates a change in the local electronic environment of Cd resulting from the interaction between Pd nanoparticles and the CdS support. The spin–orbit energy difference was found to be 6.11 eV, which corresponds to the Cd2+ state. For the S 2p spectrum, two peaks were observed at 161.5 eV and 162.8 eV, respectively, representing S 2p3/2 and S 2p1/2, with a spin–orbit separation of 1.3 eV, confirming that the compound contains sulfide species.
In the Pd 3d spectrum, two different oxidation states of palladium were observed. There were two peaks at 335.3 eV and 340.0 eV, attributed to Pd0 3d5/2 and Pd0 3d3/2, respectively, and 336.4 eV and 341.0 eV attributed to Pd2+ 3d5/2 and Pd2+ 3d3/2, respectively. Satellite peaks of two more species of palladium were detected at 337.9 eV and 342.9 eV.
## Optimization of Pd content for improved CdS catalytic activity
Photocatalytic activity increased with increasing Pd loading up to 3 wt%, but decreased gradually for higher Pd loadings greater than 3 wt%, as can be seen in Fig. 13. The effect of increasing the Pd loading on CS_Na2S was studied by increasing the Pd loading from 1 wt% to 5 wt%. It was observed that the catalytic activity increased from 1 wt% to 2 wt% and 3 wt%; i.e., for 2 wt%, the hydrogen concentration was 1587.8 µmol·g−1 and 2125.4 µmol·g−1 for 3 wt%. This can be due to the increased number of metal–semiconductor heterojunctions, but further increasing the load of Pd to 4 wt% and 5 wt% resulted in a sharp reduction of hydrogen production to 1816.8 µmol·g−1 and 1713.4 µmol g−1, respectively. This reduction of activity should be due to the aggregation of Pd nanoparticles on the surface of CdS at higher Pd loadings. Such aggregation decreases the even distribution of Pd, causing partial blockage of the active sites on CdS necessary for the absorbance of light and reactant interaction. Additionally, too high a loading of Pd may also provide recombination sites for photogenerated charge carrier pairs, thereby decreasing the photocatalytic efficiency. The apparent quantum efficiency for 3 wt% Pd/CdS was calculated to be 4.1%. The calculations are shown in the SI S4. ## Recyclability studies and spent catalyst characterizations
To determine the stability of the catalyst for long-term operation, additional experiments were performed to study the recyclability for 3 cycles of 12 h each, as shown in Fig. 14. It was observed that there was no significant change in the activity after 3 cycles, which further indicates the good stability of 3 wt% Pd/CdS_Na2S.
To directly confirm the structural stability of the catalyst after photocatalysis, post-reaction XRD and TEM (Fig. 15) analyses of the spent 3 wt% Pd–CdS catalyst were performed. The XRD patterns show that the crystal structure remains unchanged after the photocatalytic reaction, while the TEM images reveal that the morphology also remained unchanged after 3 cycles of recyclability study of 12 h each, proving the long-term stability of the photocatalyst.
## Conclusion
Different CdS catalysts with varying morphologies were synthesized using a hydrothermal method with different sulphur precursors, as well as varying structural directing agents. From this study, it was found that the use of a sulphur source and a directing agent was important for controlling the shape and structure of the synthesized CdS materials, leading to the synthesis of materials with different morphologies and physicochemical properties.
Amongst the synthesized materials, it was found that the CdS catalyst, with a spherical nanoparticle structure (CS_Na2S), showed the highest catalytic activity for the photocatalytic splitting of water to produce hydrogen. The high catalytic activity of this catalyst, with a hydrogen yield of 1009 µmol g−1, could be attributed to a number of properties, including its small particle size, high surface area, small band gap, and reduced recombination. All these properties work in conjunction to improve the catalytic efficiency.
1 wt% Pd was incorporated onto CdS via impregnation, resulting in a reduced band gap and suppressed charge recombination, which enhanced the catalytic efficiency. Despite this improvement, the relative performance trend remained unchanged, with CS_Na2S exhibiting the highest activity among all the synthesized samples. Based on this, CS_Na2S was selected to investigate the effect of increasing Pd loading. With increasing Pd content, the band gap progressively decreased, indicating enhanced visible-light absorption, while charge recombination was further suppressed, reaching a minimum at 3 wt% Pd, as confirmed by photoluminescence analysis. Consequently, the photocatalytic hydrogen production increased with Pd loading up to 3 wt%, beyond which it declined. The reduced activity at higher Pd loadings can be attributed to increased charge recombination, excessive band gap narrowing (approaching the thermodynamic limit for water splitting at ∼1.23 eV), and agglomeration of Pd particles, which reduces the number of active sites. Therefore, 3 wt% Pd was identified as the optimal loading for CdS. No significant decline in photocatalytic activity was observed over time for any of the catalysts, confirming their stable performance for the entire 12 h. Recyclability studies were also performed for 3 wt% Pd-CS_Na2S for 3 cycles of 12 h each, and the spent catalyst was characterized by XRD and TEM. No significant decrease in activity was observed. The catalyst retained its crystalline structure and morphology after the recycling study, which confirmed its good stability and durability.
