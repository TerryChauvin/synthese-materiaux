## Introduction
Water covers approximately 70% of the earth; however, only 1% of water resources are consumable[[CR1]]. It is projected that wastewater production rises 51% by 2050. In developing countries, over 90% of wastewater is directed into rivers, lakes, and coastal regions posing considerable threats to human well-being and food safety[[CR2]]. Approximately 2 million tons of sewage, industrial waste, and agricultural runoff are released daily into the world’s water bodies[[CR3]]. These pollutants include synthetic compounds, such as cosmetics, pesticides, pharmaceuticals, and personal care products[[CR4]]. Ibuprofen, atenolol, caffeine, and nonylphenol were found in wastewater treatment plant influents with average concentrations exceeding 10 μg/L[[CR4]]. The quantities of antibiotics in untreated household and pharmaceutical/hospital wastewater are 100 ng L-1 to several g L-1 and 100–500 mg L–1, respectively[[CR5]],[[CR6]]. TCs are widely prescribed antibiotics commonly used to treat infections. TCs are stable compounds, with up to 70% of their residues being discharged into water bodies[[CR7]]. TCs have been found at concentrations up to 100 mg/L in pharmaceutical and hospital wastewater effluents[[CR8]],[[CR9]], posing a significant risk to both environmental and human health. Therefore, the removal of their residue from water resources is inevitable[[CR7]]. Routine wastewater treatment techniques do not sufficiently eliminate pharmaceutical compounds; for example, atenolol, caffeine, ibuprofen, naproxen, nonylphenol, and triclosan were detected at concentrations exceeding 1 μg/L in treated effluent[[CR4]].
Current industrial wastewater treatment techniques include physical, chemical, physicochemical, and biological methods. These methods may fail depending on the source and nature of the contamination. For instance, sedimentation isolates contaminants in one phase[[CR10]]. Adsorption is often expensive and produces secondary pollutants[[CR11]]. Biological approaches utilize microorganisms to degrade contaminants, which is costly, challenging, and slow[[CR12]]. Membrane separation processes may be used to remove contaminants at low concentrations[[CR13]]; however, they are impacted by membrane fouling and costly operation[[CR14]].
Photocatalysis has emerged as a sustainable and environmentally friendly wastewater treatment technology. Photocatalysts are materials that can harness light energy to generate active species that initiate chemical reactions[[CR15]]. Photocatalytic wastewater treatment may be assisted by electrochromic materials[[CR16]], self-cleaning chemicals[[CR17]], and hydrogen generation by water splitting[[CR18]]. An efficient photocatalyst offers a substantial oxidative capability, significant solar efficiency, physiological and chemical stability, non-toxic properties and cost-effectiveness[[CR19]].
MOFs are a novel class of porous materials that arise from the coordination of metal ions or metal-containing groups with ligands. The pioneering work on MOFs was conducted by Yaghi et al.[[CR20]]. Since then, MOFs have attracted considerable interest across various fields such as chemistry, materials science, nanotechnology, catalysis, environmental science, and energy storage[[CR21]]. MOFs unique properties provide a distinct advantage for precise control of their composition, structure, topology, pore size, porosity, and surface properties[[CR22]]. A summary of MOFs and their applicability in different processes is reported in Table S1. Recent studies have shown exceptional separation capabilities of MOFs when exposed to light[[CR23]]. MOF structure may be tuned to create reactive hydrogen and oxygen species during the photocatalytic process; thus, MOFs have been effectively used to decompose antibiotics and organic dyes[[CR24]] and reduce Cr6+[[CR25]]. Various methods, such as SC, RT, HT, solvothermal, mechanochemical, and microwave irradiation-assisted can be utilized to synthesize MOFs. A comparison of different synthesis methods is summarized in Table S2. SC synthesis has gained significant interest due to its effectiveness in producing a wide range of nanomaterials[[CR26]]. This may be attributed to its remarkable capability to accomplish crystallization in a short time at a reduced reaction temperature[[CR27]]. SC synthesis requires high-intensity US waves within the frequency range of 20 kHz to 10 MHz to initiate chemical transformations of reactant molecules. SC is a liquid phase synthesis empowered by generation, enlargement, and subsequent collapse of bubbles denoted as acoustic cavitation[[CR28]] which happens in a very short time interval and substantially increases the localized thermal energy and pressure. In these temporary and confined regions of intense activity, the temperature rises to nearly 5000 K, the pressure reaches up to 1000 atmospheres, and the heating rate exceeds 1010 K/s[[CR27]].
Cerium has a wide range of applications in catalysts, adsorbents, magnetic materials, sensors and alloys[[CR29]],[[CR30]]. It is reported that Ce-based materials can be utilized as (1) catalysts for oxidation of olefins and alcohols, rhodamine B, methyl blue, Congo red, direct blue dyes[[CR31]]–[[CR35]], and urea[[CR36]], and water splitting[[CR34]]; (2) sensor for detection of telomerase activity[[CR37]], biothiols[[CR38]], H2O2[[CR31]], and ascorbic acid[[CR39]], (3) steel corrosion inhibitor[[CR40]], and (4) adsorbent for CO2[[CR41]], CH4[[CR42]], and phosphate, fluoride and arsenic species[[CR43]],[[CR44]]. Ce-MOFs have also been studied as photocatalysts for water splitting[[CR45]], and rhodamine B[[CR46]] and sulfamethoxazole degradation[[CR47]].
In this study, the Ce-MOF was successfully synthesized in controlled conditions and its synthesis condition was optimized for TC removal. Optimization of Ce-MOF synthesis was specifically focused on TC degradation due to its water solubility, persistence, and toxicity in aquatic environments[[CR48]]–[[CR50]]. The novelty of this work firstly includes selection of best Ce-MOFs synthesis method for TC degradation by examining RT, HT and SC synthesis routes. Secondly Ce-MOF was synthesized by water as a solvent instead of N,N-Dimethylformamide (DMF) which adversely affects human health[[CR51]] and environment due to its hepatotoxicity, teratogenicity, embryotoxicity, and carcinogenicity[[CR52]],[[CR53]]. Thirdly, the optimization of the Ce-MOF SC synthesis condition (initial ligand concentration, US power, and time) for sonophotocatalytic degradation of TC has been investigated for the first time.
Design Expert software (DOE) was employed to (1) optimize Ce-MOF synthesis, (2) investigate the impacts of the most influential synthesis parameters and (3) obtain the best model by the response surface methodology (RSM). Among the different categories of RSM, CCD is employed for optimizing Ce-MOF synthesis conditions due to its advantage of efficiently optimizing multiple factors with a reduced number of experiments[[CR54]]. Subsequently, the optimally synthesized sample was characterized by FT-IR, XRD, FE-SEM, TEM, particle size analysis (PSA), diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (DRS), zeta potential analysis, Mott-Schottky analysis, photocurrent analysis, electrochemical impedance spectra (EIS), and photoluminescence spectroscopy (PL). The TC mineralization efficiency of sonophotocatalytic process was calculated by comparing chemical oxygen demand (COD) of TC samples before and after experiments.
## Experimental
## Materials
Cerium nitrate (Ce(NO3)3.6H2O, 99%), benzene-1, 4-dicarboxylate (terephthalic acid/C6H4(CO2H)2,99%), ethanol absolute (CH3CH2OH, 99.9%), hydrochloride acid (HCl,37%), sodium carbonate (Na₂CO₃,99%), ammonia (NH3,25%), Ethylenedinitrilotetraacetic acid disodium salt dihydrate (EDTA, 99%), benzoquinone (BQ, 99%), and isopropanol (IPA, 99%) were purchased from Merck Company, Germany. Analytical grade tetracycline hydrochloride was generously provided by Sinadarou Labs Company. Deionized water (DI) was used as the solvent.
## Synthesis of Ce-MOF
To optimize the Ce-MOF synthesis toward enhanced TC adsorption and photodegradation, different synthesis methods, namely RT, HT, and SC, were studied as outlined in Fig. 1.
## Room temperature synthesis
Ce-MOF was synthesized at RT by adding 70 mg terephthalic acid as ligand to 50 mL DI under stirring. Ammonia solution (46 wt.%) was then gradually added to the solution to increase pH to 7. Afterward, 10 mL of 27.6 mM Ce(NO3)3 aqueous solution was gradually added to the solution, producing white Ce-MOF precipitates. The precipitate was stirred for 12 h to ensure uniform mixing. It was then collected by centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 5 min at RT. Ce-MOF precipitate was subsequently washed with DI and ethanol. Finally, it was dried at 60 °C for 24 h[[CR55]].
## Hydrothermal synthesis
The HT Ce-MOF synthesis is similar to RT synthesis. The only difference is that in the HT method, the final solution is placed in a high-pressure hydrothermal reactor. The reactor was then placed in an oven at 120 °C for 120 h[[CR56]],[[CR57]]. Finally, the precipitate was collected, washed, and dried according to the RT synthesis procedure.
## Sonochemical synthesis
SC synthesis utilizes ultrasound irradiation. Herein, an appropriate amount of terephthalic acid was added to 50 mL of DI while stirring to achieve a final concentration of 7.4–11.4 mmol/L. Ammonia solution (46 wt.%) was gradually added to the solution to increase pH to 7. Then, an ultrasound probe (QSONICA, Q700) was placed in the solution at ambient conditions. 10 mL of Ce(NO3)3 aqueous solution was gradually added into the solution to achieve a final terephthalic acid to Ce(NO3)3 molar ratio equal to 1.5. Power output and US time were in the range of 45–65 amplitude and 30–180 min, respectively. The ligand concentration and US time/power were chosen as input parameters for the CCD. Finally, the precipitate was collected, washed, and dried according to the RT synthesis procedure.
## Ce-MOF characterization
FTIR Spectroscopy was performed in the range of 400–4000 cm-1. X-ray diffraction was conducted using graphite-monochromatic Cu Kα radiation at a wavelength of 1.54056 Å at 25 °C by Bruker Tensor II and Bruker D8 Advance diffractometer with a scanning rate of 0.02°min-1 at 2θ = 10–80°. Sample morphology was obtained by TEM (Philips EM 208S) and FE-SEM (TESCAN; MIRA III) with an acceleration voltage of 100 keV. UV–vis-NIR diffuse reflectance spectra were performed using Holmarc HO-SP-DRSP100 spectrophotometers at the wavelength (λmax) of 360 nm, which corresponds to the maximum UV absorption of TC. Zeta potential analysis was performed by Horiba SZ-100 at RT. Herein, 100 mg of Ce-MOF was dispersed in 100 mL DI using an ultrasound bath for 5 min. To determine the surface charge and locate the isoelectric point, suspensions with different pHs were prepared using 0.1 M solutions of HCl and NaOH. PL data were collected using a Varian Spectrophotometer in the wavelength range of 200–800 nm under 325 nm excitation. Photoelectrochemical and electrochemical characteristic of Ce-MOF were determined using Metrohm Dropsens µstat-i400s instrument, employing the traditional three-electrode system to obtain Mott-Schottky, photocurrent and EIS analyses. In brief, 3 mg of Ce-MOF was dispersed in 10 μL DI and subjected to ultrasonication for 5 min. An Ag/AgCl electrode and a graphite wire were used as the reference and counter electrodes, respectively. A 0.2 mol/L Na2SO4 aqueous solution was used as the electrolyte for measurements.
## Sonophotocatalytic TC degradation experiments
Sonophotocatalytic TC degradation assisted by Ce-MOF was performed in a batch sonophotocatalyst reactor. The irradiation source (UV-A light, OSRAM ULTRA-MED FDA 400W) was placed 10 cm away of the reaction solution. The experiments were conducted in a 50 mL glass beaker placed in the reactor at 25 °C. To enhance the mixing and aeration of the TC solution, the beaker was placed in an ultrasound bath (50W). Air was blown into the chamber to eliminate the lamp’s heating effect. The temperature of the ultrasound water bath was maintained at 298K by circulating its water content.
To establish TC adsorption–desorption equilibrium on the Ce-MOF photocatalyst, the dispersion was left at dark environment for 60 min. During the photocatalytic degradation experiment, up to 3 mL of the reaction mixture were extracted, centrifuged and filtered using a syringe filter (pore size < 0.22 μm) to eliminate Ce-MOF particles in the solution. The samples were subsequently analyzed using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer to determine their TC concentration. The removal efficiency was calculated using Eq. ## Experimental design and optimization process
Experimental design assists in identifying the interplay of essential parameters within specified ranges by minimum number of experiments. The subsequent analysis of response variance provides a suitable model that can be utilized to identify the optimum synthesis condition[[CR58]]. In this study the CCD RSM was employed to investigate the impact of ligand concentration and US time/power interplay on TC removal efficiency. Table S3 shows the effective parameters and their ranges. Table S4 shows conditions of different experiments.
## Trapping experiments
To investigate the impact of each active species on sonophotocatalytic activity of Ce-MOF, free radical trapping experiments were conducted. IPA, EDTA, and BQ were employed to quench h+, ⋅OH, and ⋅O2− during TC sonophotocatalytic degradation, respectively. The corresponding TC degradation experiments were conducted in an aqueous solution containing an initial TC concentration of 120 ppm, 1 g/L Ce-MOF, and 0.3 mM trapping agent at initial pH of 7.
## Results and discussion
## Selection of Ce-MOF synthesis method
TC degradation by Ce-MOFs synthesized via RT, HT, and SC methods was performed at pH = 7, initial TC concentration of 120 ppm, photocatalyst concentration of 1 g/L, and degradation time of 120 min. According to Table S4, the Ce-MOF synthesized by the SC method shows the highest TC removal of 82.5%. Thus, further synthesis optimization was performed on Ce-MOF via the SC method.

## Evaluation of model provided by DOEs method
Table S5 presents statistical parameters obtained to evaluate the accuracy of the model. Parameters such as SD, R-square (R2), Adjusted R-square (RAdj), predicted R-square (Rpred), and adequate precision (PAdeq) are employed for model evaluation. Notably, Rpred, RAdj, and R2 values approach 1, while the PAdeq value surpasses 4, confirming model accuracy[[CR59]]. Additionally, Fig. 2a illustrates the residual values plotted against the run number. The data points in Fig. 2a lack a specific trend and show a random distribution with a roughly equal number of points above and below the x-axis. This indicates that residual values are not influenced by the run number. Figure 2b shows residuals vs. predicted values where data are randomly scattered around the zero line. Such trend further confirms constant variance and model adequacy. The plot of predicted vs. actual degradation in Fig. 2c shows that the experimental results closely match the model predictions further confirming model accuracy.
## Effect of operating parameters on synthesis
Figure 3 reveals effect of synthesis parameters on TC degradation efficiency. It can be seen from Fig. 3a that TC degradation depends on the interplay of US time and power. For a given US power an optimum US time exists and vice versa. For example, it can be seen from Fig. 3d that at constant US power of 55 amplitude and ligand concentration of 9.4 mmol/L, TC degradation initially rises as US time increases due to enhanced molecular collisions, which promotes formation of the desired bond; however, after ~ 90 min, TC degradation efficiency declines probably due to Ce-MOF particle agglomeration[[CR60]]. Figure 3b, c, and e indicate that TC degradation steadily decreases by increasing ligand concentration. According to Fig. 3c, increasing US power at low ligand concentration reduces TC degradation efficiency as it reduces effective collision time which hinders particle growth. On the other hand, at high ligand concentrations, increasing sonication power results in enhanced TC degradation as it suppresses particle agglomeration and promotes particle dispersion[[CR61]]. According to Fig. 3f, moderate US power is optimum at moderate ligand concentration.
## Optimization and its validation
Numerical optimization techniques embedded in the DOE were utilized to find synthesis conditions that maximize the TC degradation. The optimum synthesis conditions are US time of 60 min, US power of 50 amplitudes, and ligand concentration of 8.4 mmol/L. The predicted TC degradation at optimum synthesis condition is 81.75%. To validate the predicted optimum condition, three independent experiments, whose outcomes are shown in Fig. S1, were carried. The experimentally obtained TC degradation at optimum condition is 81.05 ± 0.74%, which suggests a slight error of 0.86% in predicting the optimum TC degradation by the model.

## Fourier-Transform Infrared Spectroscopy
The presence of functional groups in Ce-MOFs was studied using FTIR analysis. FTIR spectra of synthesized Ce-MOFs through various methods, Ce-MOF spectra from literature[[CR56]], and terephthalic acid spectra are shown in Fig. 4a. The peak located at the region of 511 cm-1 of Ce-MOFs spectra, synthesized via RT, HT, and SC methods, is attributed to the Ce–O stretching vibration[[CR55]]. The peaks at 730–770 cm-1 region indicate the ortho, meta, and para position substituted groups in the benzene ring. The clear peaks within the 1000–1200 cm−1 range correspond to the stretching vibration of C–O bonds[[CR62]]. The peaks observed at 1400–1540 cm-1 indicate O–H bending. The stretching of carbonyl groups (C=O) and carboxylate anions can be seen at 1550–1600 cm-1. C=C stretching peak occurred at 1600–1700 cm-1. The peaks at 2900–3000 and 3400–3500 cm-1 correspond to aromatic C-H stretching and OH groups, respectively[[CR56]]. The distinctive bands of the nonionized carboxyl group of terephthalic acid (νC=O: 1690 cm-1; δC=O: 528 cm-1; ν-OH: 3100 cm-1) vanish in the Ce-MOF. Furthermore, the peak of ν-OH around 3465 cm-1 confirms the incorporation of water molecules into the Ce-MOFs[[CR63]].

## Zeta potential
Zeta potential analysis was conducted at various pHs to assess the surface charge and stability of the Ce-MOF nanostructure. Figure 7a illustrates the average zeta potential values versus the pH. The result shows that the Ce-MOF has an isoelectric point at pH 5. Moreover, TC has three acid dissociation constants (pKa) of 3.3, 7.7, and 9.7[[CR65]]. In acidic conditions (pH < 3.3), TC predominantly exists in its cationic form. At 3.3 < pH < 7.7, TC is in the zwitterionic state, and at pH > 7.7 it is in the anionic form. Thus, the photocatalyst carries slightly negative charges during TC degradation experiments (pH = 7) while TC is in the zwitterionic state; consequently, it is electrostatically favorable that TC molecules orient around the Ce-MOF so that their negative moiety stay away.
## Photoluminescence spectroscopy (PL)
The electronic structure of the synthesized Ce-MOF nanoparticles was analyzed using PL and illustrated in Fig. 7b. This spectra predominantly features a peak in the UV region (below 400 nm), demonstrating an effective UV response. Distinct UV emission peaks at 380 (3.17 eV) and 395 nm (3.04 eV) and a visible emission peak at 435 nm (2.77 eV) are observed. The UV emission peak at 395 nm is attributed to near-band edge (NBE) emission caused by excitonic recombination[[CR66]],[[CR67]]. The emission bands ranging from 358 to 450 nm are due to oxygen vacancies (surface defects) in the Ce-MOF nanoparticles, which may affect particle size. These oxygen vacancies can also be explained by the charge carrier transfer from the 4f. conduction band of Ce to the 2p valence band of O in the Ce-MOF nanoparticles[[CR68]],[[CR69]].

## Electrochemical measurements (photocurrent, Mott-Schottky and EIS)
Transient photocurrent measurements were used to analyze interfacial charge separation and transmission dynamics as depicted in Fig. 7d. A higher photocurrent intensity indicates better separation efficiency of electron–hole pairs[[CR70]],[[CR71]]. During light irradiation, a gradual increase in photocurrent is attributed to electron trapping by oxygen vacancies, enhancing electron–hole separation and improving photocatalytic performance, as supported by photoluminescence (PL) results. The photocurrent density of Ce-MOF reached 114 μA cm−2 (approximately 3 times higher than that of pure CeO2[[CR72]]). The decreasing trend of photocurrent curve in Fig. 7d due to intermittent turn on and turn off of the light is a characteristic of semiconducting photocatalysts.
The Mott–Schottky test determines the type of semiconductor and the position of its valence band (VB) and conduction band (CB). The positive slope in in Fig. 7e revealed that Ce-MOF is n-type semiconductor[[CR73]]. According to Fig. 7d the flat band potential (EFB) of Ce-MOF is − 0.25 eV (vs Ag/AgCl). It is known that the conduction band potential (ECB) of n-type semiconductors is approximately 0.10 eV lower than their EFB[[CR73]]; thus, ECB of Ce-MOF is − 0.35 eV (vs Ag/AgCl). The valance band potential (EVB) of the samples was calculated as 2.59 eV using the samples’ bandgap data (Eg = 2.94 eV) of Tauc plot depicted in Fig. 7d[[CR73]].
The electrochemical impedance spectra (EIS) Nyquist plot in Fig. 7f resembles a semicircle whose radius reflects the charge transfer process. The radius of the semicircle for Ce-MOF is relatively small (compared to that of CeO2[[CR72]]), highlighting enhanced efficiency of transferring photoexcited electrons from holes. Deviations from the semicircular shape could occur due to fast electron transfer processes within the material[[CR74]]. Therefore, the Ce-MOF exhibits accelerated separation of photogenerated electron–hole pairs, leading to enhanced photocatalytic activity.
## TC degradation experiments
## Mineralization efficiency
Mineralization efficiency of TC degradation by Ce-MOF was assessed by COD measurements before and after the process. Each sample underwent oxidation in test tubes containing potassium dichromate (K₂Cr₂O₇) under acidic conditions, followed by heating to ensure complete oxidation. Oxygen consumption was measured using a spectrophotometer. COD of TC samples dropped from 118.5 mg/L to 34.1 mg/L upon its sonophotocatalytic degradation which correspond to a mineralization efficiency of 71%. This outcome underscores the robust activity of the Ce-MOF in degrading TC affirming its potential for effective organic pollutant removal from wastewater.
## Trapping experiments
The primary agents responsible for the degradation process are active intermediates such as ⋅O2−, ⋅OH, and h+[[CR75]]. A stronger inhibitory effect of a trapping agent (scavenger) indicates a more significant role of the corresponding free radical in the degradation reaction[[CR76]],[[CR77]]. As illustrated in Fig. S2b, the presence of 0.3 mM EDTA, BQ, and IPA notably reduced the degradation rate of TC. The inhibitory effects of these trapping agents were in the order: BQ > EDTA > IPA, with TC degradation rates of 47.67%, 64.96%, and 77.91%, respectively. Significant reduction of sonophotodegradation efficiency upon addition of BQ and EDTA, suggests that ⋅O2− is the most crucial active radical. Minimal impact of IPA, indicates that ⋅OH does not actively promote TC degradation.
The sonophotocatalytic degradation mechanism of the Ce-MOF is proposed in Fig. S4, when the Ce-MOF is simultaneously exposed to ultrasound and light, electrons transition from the valence band to the conduction band, leaving holes in the valence band. The conduction band position of the Ce-MOF is − 0.35 eV, which is more negative than the O2/O2− (− 0.33 eV) reduction potential, allowing photogenerated electrons to react with oxygen to form superoxide radicals (⋅O2− ). The valence band (VB) of the Ce-MOF is + 2.59 eV, which is less positive than the OH⁻/⋅OH (1.99 eV) oxidation potential, leading to the generation of ⋅OH through reactions of holes (h+) with H₂O. The π-π conjugate structure present in the organic ligand of Ce-MOF facilitates the rapid transfer of photogenerated electrons from holes to the Ce center site of the Ce-MOF, where they react with adsorbed TC. Photogenerated electrons and holes on the Ce-MOF surface oxidize O₂ and H₂O into ⋅O2− and ⋅OH[[CR46]]. These free radicals ultimately oxidize TC molecules to produce CO₂ and H₂O. ## Reusability and stability of Ce-MOF
Chemical and structural stability of catalyst are essential for a durable and cost-effective sonophotocatalytic process. The stability and reusability of Ce-MOF were assessed during four consecutive TC degradation cycles. The corresponding TC degradation experiments were conducted in an aqueous solution containing an initial TC concentration of 120 ppm at initial pH of 7. The initial Ce-MOF concentration was 1 g/L. After each cycle, Ce-MOF was separated by centrifugation, washed by ethanol and DI, and reused. The change in TC removal efficiency as a function of Ce-MOF recycle time is depicted in Fig. S2a. It can be seen from Fig. S2a that Ce-MOF retains 80% of its activity upon 3 cycles. Additionally, comparison of Fig. 4b and S3 reveals that Ce-MOF retains its XRD patterns after recycle. These results confirm the stability and reusability Ce-MOF.
## Conclusion
In this work, the Ce-MOF nanostructure was synthesized using RT, HT, and SC methods. SC, a non-conventional heating technique, was selected as the optimal synthesis method since it demonstrated superiority as manifested by its highest TC removal efficiency. CCD experimental design was employed to investigate the impact of the synthesis condition of Ce-MOF on its sonophotocatalytic activity. FTIR, XRD, and EDS analyses confirmed Ce-MOF chemical and crystalline structure. The FE-SEM, TEM, and PSA studies indicated that nanoparticles were formed. Photocurrent, Mott-Schottky and EIS provided detailed information regarding Ce-MOF photocatalytic properties. The DRS and PL analysis further confirmed Ce-MOF photocatalytic activity in the UV region. The RSM revealed the synthesis conditions that resulted in the maximum TC removal. Optimally synthesized Ce-MOF has the potential to degrade 81.75% of TC within 120 min in an aqueous solution containing an initial TC concentration of 120 ppm and a photocatalyst concentration of 1 g/L at an initial pH of 7. The COD elimination demonstrated that the optimally synthesized Ce-MOF sonophotocatalyst was highly effective, achieving 71% TC mineralization efficiency. Additionally, Ce-MOF retains its chemical stability and can be recycled making it an ideal choice for removing non-biodegradable pharmaceuticals from wastewater. It should be noted that in this study only the Ce-MOF synthesis condition were optimized and optimization of TC degradation condition is ongoing. The results of this study can be utilized in future studies to model or scale up pharmaceutical, municipal and hospital wastewater treatment facilities.
