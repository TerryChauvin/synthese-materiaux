## Introduction
Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the most common primary malignancy of the liver and ranks as the third leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [, ]. The global burden of HCC continues to rise, particularly in regions with high prevalence of chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) and hepatitis C virus (HCV) infections []. Despite advances in surveillance programs for high-risk populations, the majority of HCC cases are diagnosed at advanced stages, limiting the curative treatment options []. A significant proportion of patients present with intermediate or advanced-stage disease, for which systemic therapies are the mainstay of treatment. Therefore, to enhance the efficacy of HCC treatment, a combination of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) and trans-arterial chemoembolization (TACE) has demonstrated synergistic effect in anti-tumor activity, with several clinical trials currently underway [–]. To further prevent and protect against malignant tumors, adopting healthy dietary habits and implementing clinical diagnostic risk stratification may have a positive impact on patients [, ].
In recent years, natural products have been widely utilized in drug development and research due to their structural diversity and biological activities [, ]. Among these, Tripterygium wilfordii has emerged as a focal point of study and is commonly used in the treatment of rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus, nephrotic syndrome, and various malignancies []. However, the complex and diverse chemical constituents of Tripterygium wilfordii have been shown to cause significant toxicity to the hematologic, reproductive, and gastrointestinal systems during treatment [, ]. To address these issues, researchers worldwide have sought to isolate and purify the active components from Tripterygium wilfordii, aiming to enhance therapeutic efficacy while minimizing adverse effects [, ]. Celastrol (Cel), a pentacyclic triterpenoid compound extracted from Tripterygium wilfordii, has been recognized as one of the top five natural compounds with the most potential for drug development []. Studies have demonstrated that celastrol can effectively inhibit the proliferation, invasion, and metastasis of cancer cells, including breast cancer, prostate cancer, and leukemia, by modulating multiple cellular signaling pathways []. Nevertheless, the clinical application of celastrol is significantly hindered for its poor water solubility, low bioavailability, high toxicity, and narrow therapeutic window, which present major challenges to its drug-ability [].
The development of nanotechnology has promoted innovative approaches for the diagnosis and treatment of modern medicine, providing a range of nanoparticles for biomedical research, including quantum dots (QDs), dendrimers, metals, metal oxides, and silicon- and carbon-based nanoparticles []. The 2023 Nobel Prize in Chemistry was awarded to Moungi G. Bawendi, Louis E. Brus, and Alexei I. Ekimov for the discovery of QDs, highlighting the immense potential of QDs in the future []. A novel drug delivery system comprising anti-CD22-conjugated cadmiu-tellurium quantum dots (CdTe QDs) co-loaded with doxorubicin and gambogic acid (as a chemosensitizer).” So, nanoparticle-based targeted chemotherapy shows specific advantages []. Therefore, the development of novel nanocarrier drug delivery systems for targeted delivery of celastrol will have great prospects for clinical application. Li et al. [] reported a pH-responsive bufalin-containing CaCO3 nanoparticles (BCNPs) calcium alginate hydrogel (BCNPs@gel), which can enhance cancer therapy efficacy by inducing cellular heat shock. Zhang et al. [] combined selenium nanoparticles (SeNPs) with heteropolysaccharide (MTP70) to create a novel stable nanoparticle (MTP-SeNP), which effectively inhibits the proliferation, invasion, and metastasis of HepG2 cells by inducing apoptosis. Pooresmaeil et al. [] reported a composite (CS/CUR@GQDs@MIL-88(Fe)) formed by porous MIL-88 (Fe), graphene quantum dots (GQDs), curcumin (CUR), and chitosan (CS). Although the encapsulation efficiency was only 5.36%, the composite enhanced safety and improved anti-tumor efficacy. Nano-delivery systems can not only improve drug pharmacokinetics and biodistribution but also increase the concentration of the drug at the target, thereby enhancing anti-tumor efficacy while reducing toxicity.
QDs are nanoscale semiconductor materials with sizes typically ranging from 2 to 10 nm. Their key features are the tunable emission wavelength resulting from quantum size effects, coupled with exceptional optical properties such as narrow emission spectra, broad absorption spectra, and high photostability [, ]. These optical advantages of QDs have enabled to be widely used in various life sciences applications, including cellular labeling and imaging. Moreover, QDs can form nano-drug complexes by coupling with drug molecules through electrostatic or covalent interactions. These complexes allow for drug molecule tracking in vitro or in vivo while maintaining good biocompatibility and low cytotoxicity [, ]. In the past few decades, most QDs were composed of heavy metals, such as Cd, Hg, and Pb. However, the potential toxicity of heavy metals released during QDs degradation has limited their biomedical applications []. More recently, heavy metal-free ternary I-III-VI QDs, such as AgInS₂, CuInS₂, and CuInSe₂, have been extensively developed in biomedical field due to their unique optical properties and low toxicity. Among them, AgInS₂ QDs, with their more stable Ag⁺ and S2⁻ ionic precursors compared to Cu⁺, Se2⁻, and Te2⁻, have shown promise as biocompatible nanomaterials widely applied in tumor research []. Additionally, more studies have utilized AgInS₂ QDs for biomolecular detection and drug quantification. For instance, Yang et al. demonstrated that ciprofloxacin effectively quenched the fluorescence of AgInS₂ QDs, leading to the development of a fluorescence quenching-based method for quantifying ciprofloxacin content in pharmaceuticals []. Although reports on the biomedical applications of AgInS₂ QDs are increasingly prevalent, their potential in anti-tumor drug delivery remains largely underexplored and requires further in-depth investigation.
Therefore, this study aimed to develop a nanocarrier drug delivery system based on AgInS₂ QDs to relieve the limitations of celastrol, including low bioavailability and high toxicity. This approach was expected to enhance the selectivity of celastrol against tumor cells, providing a solid theoretical foundation for the clinical application of celastrol and the broader adoption of AgInS₂ QDs based nanocarrier systems.
## Materials and methods
## Synthesis of AgInS₂ quantum dots (AIS QDs)
Based on previous literature [], AIS QDs were synthesized using a simple aqueous-phase reflux method with polyethyleneimine (PEI) as a stabilizing agent. Firstly, 2 mL of AgNO₃ (catalog # S116267, Aladdin Biochemical Technology, China), 18 mL of InCl₃ (catalog # I302881, Aladdin Biochemical Technology, China), and 10 mL of water were added to a 100 mL three-necked flask and stirred. Subsequently, 0.6 g of PEI (catalog # E107079, Aladdin Biochemical Technology, China) was added to the mixture and stirred at room temperature for 0.5 h. Next, 3 mL of 0.2 M Na₂S solution (catalog # S116078, Aladdin Biochemical Technology, China) was dropped into the flask. The reaction mixture was heated to 90 °C and stirred for 2 h to promote the growth of QDs. After the reaction, absolute ethanol was added to the mixture, and the solution was centrifuged to collect the AIS QDs precipitate. The AIS QDs precipitate was washed three times with absolute ethanol, followed by three additional washes with deionized water. Finally, the purified AIS QDs were dried overnight in a vacuum oven at 70 °C.
## Preparation of AIS QD and celastrol complex (AIS QD@Cel)
25 mg of AIS QDs and 1 mg of celastrol (catalog # BD33290, Bide Pharmatech, China) were added to a single-necked flask and dissolved in 100 µL of DMSO and 2 mL of water. The mixture was stirred overnight at room temperature in dark. The resulting solution was then centrifuged at 3000 rpm to collect the precipitate. The precipitate was washed three times with deionized water and then dried in a vacuum oven. The final product AIS QD@Cel was obtained.
## Characterizations
The microstructure, including particle size and shape, of AIS QDs was characterized by Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) using a JEM 2100plus (JEOL, Japan). Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectrometry of AIS QDs, celastrol, and AIS QD@Cel was performed on a Nicolet iS20 (Thermo Scientific, USA) to facilitate the identification of the synthesis and binding mode of the AIS QD@Cel complex. The UV–Visible absorption spectrum of celastrol was measured using a K5800 (Kaiao, China) to assess the encapsulation efficiency of AIS QD@Cel.
## Cytotoxicity evaluation
The cytotoxicity assay was measured in a CCK-8 (Cell Counting Kit-8) colorimetric assay. The CCK-8 kit was purchased from Abbkine (catalog # BMU106-CN, Abbkine, China). The cell lines (293T, Hep3B, and HepG2) used in the experiment were initially obtained from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC Manassas, VA) and cultured in DMEM containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (P/S) at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. Cells were seeded at an appropriate density in a 96-well plate and allowed to grow in the exponential phase. After 48 h of incubation with AIS QD@Cel in the incubator, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well and incubated for an additional 2–4 h. All tested compounds were assessed at more than five concentration gradients and repeated three times. The absorbance values (OD) were measured at 450 nm (detection wavelength) and 650 nm (reference wavelength) using a BioTek multimode microplate reader, and the inhibition rates were calculated. Concentration at 50% inhibition (IC50) of each tested compound was derived by a nonlinear fitting of the average inhibition rates measured at different doses.
## Western blot
Cells were treated with AIS QD@Cel and Cel at concentrations of 0.2, 0.4, and 2 μM for 24 h, then lysed in ice-cold RIPA lysis buffer (catalog # P0013B, Beyotime, China). The lysate samples were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C to collect the supernatants. Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA protein assay kit. The samples were heated at 100 °C for 10 min and resolved on 12.5% SDS polyacrylamide gels using a Bio-Rad electrophoresis apparatus. Proteins were transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, USA) using a Bio-Rad Trans-Blot Turbo electrotransfer system. The membranes were blocked for 1 h at room temperature with 5% nonfat milk in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST). The membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against PARP (catalog # 13371-1-AP, Proteintech, China), BAX (catalog # 50599-2-Ig, Proteintech, China), caspase-3 (catalog # 19677-1-AP, Proteintech, China), and GAPDH (catalog # 10494-1-AP, Proteintech, China), each at a 1:1000 dilution in TBST with 5% nonfat milk. After washing three times with TBST, the membranes were incubated for 2 h at room temperature with a 1:4000 dilution of horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-mouse or goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody. The membranes were washed three more times with TBST and protein expression was detected using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagents.
## Flow cytometry
Cells were incubated with AIS QD@Cel at concentrations of 0.05, 0.2, 0.8 and 3.2 μM in a six-well cell culture plate for 12 h. Each sample was collected, centrifuged and washed with PBS, and dispersed in 100 μL Annexin V binding buffer. Each sample was stained with 5 μL Annexin V-FITC and 5 μL propidium iodide staining solution, respectively, in dark at the room temperature for 15 min. All samples were analyzed using a BD Biosciences flow cytometer.
## RNA-seq assay
After rapidly washing cancer cells with PBS, total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol method and transferred to an RNase-free tube and frozen with liquid nitrogen rapidly. To ensure the quality of the RNA samples, RNA purity (OD260/280 and OD260/230) was assessed using a NanoPhotometer spectrophotometer, RNA concentration was accurately quantified using a Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer, and RNA integrity was evaluated using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. Subsequently, an RNA library was constructed using the TruSeqTM RNA Sample Preparation Kit, and high-throughput sequencing was performed on an Illumina HiSeq 10/NovaSeq platform with the 6000-sequencing system. Differential gene sets were subjected to KEGG pathway enrichment analysis using the clusterProfiler software. This analysis aimed to identify the differential effects of AIS QD@Cel and Cel.
## Statistical analysis
The results are presented as means ± standard deviations, at least three samples. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0.
## Results and discussions
## Synthesis and characterization
A straightforward method was employed to conjugate Cel with AIS QD, resulting in the formation of AIS QD@Cel. Notably, in vitro cellular experiments demonstrated that AIS QD@Cel exhibited low toxicity in 293T cell line compared to Cel, while showing enhanced efficacy for HCC. TEM was used to observe the quantum dot nanoparticles. TEM image of AIS QDs revealed uniform dispersion in water without significant aggregation (Fig. 1a). The quantum dots had diameters ranging from 2 to 4 nm, demonstrating favorable physicochemical properties. Subsequently, the optical properties of AIS QDs were characterized using UV–vis absorption spectroscopy, as shown in Fig. 1. The UV–Vis absorption spectrum (Fig. 1b) revealed a strong absorption peak around 300 nm within the wavelength range of 300–700 nm.
The interaction between AIS QDs and Cel was confirmed through Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR). The FTIR of AIS QDs, Cel, and AIS QD@Cel are shown in Fig. 2. The results indicated that the characteristic carboxyl peaks of Cel (3205 cm⁻1 and 1727 cm⁻1) underwent a reaction with the amine groups of AIS QDs (3200–3500 cm⁻1 and 1610 cm⁻1), forming an amide bond (1702 cm⁻1). This demonstrated that Cel was successfully conjugated to the surface of the QDs.
Subsequently, the encapsulation efficiency of AIS QD@Cel was evaluated. A full-wavelength UV spectrum scan of Cel revealed a maximum absorption peak at 420 nm, which was used to determine its concentration. After synthesis and centrifugation of AIS QD@Cel, the residual Cel in the supernatant liquid was quantified to calculate the amount of Cel encapsulated by AIS QDs, yielding an encapsulation efficiency of 27.5%. These results demonstrated that the AIS QD exhibited a favorable encapsulation efficiency for Cel, surpassing the commonly reported encapsulation rates of 10–20% for this heavy metal-free element []. AIS QD@Cel successfully overcomes the toxicity problem associated with traditional QDs, and exhibits a high drug-loading capacity, making it suitable for therapeutic applications. However, research on its distribution, metabolism, and excretion mechanisms in vivo remains insufficient, with a lack of long-term animal studies to evaluate its safety. Moreover, the production of QDs still faces challenges such as high costs and difficulty in scaling up, which limits their widespread clinical adoption. With technological advancements, future developments may integrate QDs with other cutting-edge technologies (such as gene editing and artificial intelligence), facilitating the personalization, precision, and efficiency of material-drug combinations.
## Cytotoxicity on HCC
Previous studies have demonstrated that Cel can effectively inhibit the proliferation of HCC. However, its poor water solubility and high toxicity limit its clinical potential. To evaluate the anti-HCC activity and cytotoxicity of AIS QD@Cel, concentration-dependent cytotoxicity assays were performed, as shown in Fig. 3. AIS QDs exhibited no cytotoxicity against 293T, HepG2, or Hep3B cell lines, with IC50 values exceeding 100 µM. AIS QD@Cel exhibited significantly enhanced safety compared to Cel in 293T cells, with the IC50 increasing from 1.84 μM (Cel) to 20.82 μM (AIS QD@Cel). Additionally, AIS QD@Cel demonstrated over a tenfold increase in cytotoxicity against Hep3B and HepG2 cell lines, compared to Cel alone (IC50: 0.33 vs 5.79 μM for Hep3B and 0.56 vs 5.79 μM for HepG2). These findings indicate that AIS QD@Cel not only significantly improves the therapeutic safety margin of Cel but also enhances its anti-cancer efficacy. Currently, we have only tested the cytotoxicity on a subset of liver cancer cell lines, and the response may vary significantly in other cell types. Further studies are needed to expand the evaluation to a broader range of tumor cell lines, providing a more comprehensive assessment of biological activity.
## Investigation of the mechanism of action for AIS QD@Cel
We conducted a mechanism study of AIS QD@Cel using Hep3B cell line as the research model. After co-incubating Hep3B with different concentrations of AIS QD@Cel and Cel for 24 h, we performed western blot analysis and flow cytometry experiment. AIS QD@Cel effectively promoted cell apoptosis in a dose-dependent manner. Key apoptosis-related proteins, such as full-caspase 3 and full-PARP, generated cleaved-caspase 3 and cleaved-PARP in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 4), and these results were further supported by flow cytometry. The combination of annexin V and PI can distinguish cells in early apoptosis [annexin V (+) and PI (−)] or late apoptosis [annexin V (+) and PI (+)], corresponding to the lower left and upper right regions in these two figures. In this experiment, AIS QD@Cel was incubated with Hep3B cells at different doses (0.05, 0.2, 0.8, 3.2 µM), and induced late apoptosis in a clear dose-dependent manner (Fig. 5). Additionally, AIS QD@Cel increased the expression of the pro-apoptotic protein BAX in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 4). At higher concentrations of AIS QD@Cel, both apoptosis and autophagy were observed in Hep3B, which may be a potential mechanism underlying its high cytotoxicity.
Finally, we performed transcriptome sequencing analysis on Hep3B cells treated with AIS QD@Cel and Cel. The KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that AIS QD@Cel, compared to Cel, significantly enriched tumor-related pathways (Fig. 6a). Additionally, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) analysis shown several genes associated with tumor adhesion, invasion, proliferation, and metastasis were identified, including RAC2, LAMB3, WNT7B, RASGRP2, ITGA3, NOTCH3, and GNAI1 (Fig. 6b), which could serve as potential targets for drug discovery and therapeutic intervention. RAC2, LAMB3 and ITGA3 are known to contribute to the metastatic potential of cancer cells by promoting tumor cell invasion and metastasis. The differential expressions in response to AIS QD@Cel suggest that the compound may hinder cell motility and metastatic spread by modulating those gens activity. RASGRP2 and NOTCH3 have been implicated in the promotion of tumor cell proliferation and resistance to apoptosis. Their modulation by AIS QD@Cel could indicate a mechanism for reducing the survival and proliferation of cancer cells. GNAI1 is a member of the G protein family that is involved in various signaling pathways, including those controlling cell proliferation, migration, and survival. In the anti-HCC activity of AIS QD@Cel compared to Cel, GNAI1 also plays an important regulatory role.
While the study provides valuable insights into the apoptosis pathway, further research is needed to explore how these processes are regulated at the molecular level and to determine if other cell death pathways, such as necroptosis, play a role. In the future, we will identify and validate the role of the differentially expressed genes in tumor progression and metastasis, and evaluate their potential as therapeutic targets.
## Conclusions
In summary, Cel exhibits potential antitumor activity, its strong toxicity to normal cells limits its clinical application potential. Currently, most research efforts focus on structural modifications to reduce its toxicity and enhance its tumor inhibition. With the rapid development of heavy metal-free QDs, utilizing the biological properties of QDs, such as high biocompatibility, low cytotoxicity, high cell permeability, and targeting ability, holds promise for effectively expanding the therapeutic potential of Cel. In this study, we synthesized AIS QD and successfully encapsulated Cel on their surface via amide bonds, forming AIS QD@Cel. The AIS QD@Cel exhibited favorable nanostructural properties, drug-loading capacity, and cellular selectivity. It effectively reduced the biotoxicity on 293T cell line while enhancing the inhibitory effect on Hep3B and HepG2 cell lines, thereby increasing the safety threshold for Cel application. This approach enables the design of AIS QDs as an effective drug delivery vehicle for cancer therapy to expand the clinical application scope of the drugs. Meanwhile, it expands the potential applications of certain natural medicine. However, there are several limitations to this study that need to be addressed in future research. First, while the in vitro results are promising, we have yet to evaluate the in vivo safety, bio-distribution, and long-term effects of AIS QD@Cel in animal models. Further studies should include pharmacokinetics and toxicity assessments to better understand the systemic effects and organ-specific interactions. Second, the lack of comprehensive clinical trials limits the generalization of our findings, and future clinical studies will be necessary to confirm the therapeutic efficacy and safety of AIS QD@Cel in patients.
As we look ahead, the next five years may see significant advancements in nanomedicine, particularly with the integration of quantum dots in drug delivery systems. We anticipate that advancements in surface modification techniques will allow for better control over drug release profiles, targeting specificity, and minimizing off-target effects. Furthermore, the combination of quantum dots with emerging technologies such as CRISPR-based gene editing or artificial intelligence may allow for personalized, precise cancer therapies. Overcoming challenges related to the scalability of quantum dot production, cost reduction, and improving in vivo compatibility will be critical for advancing this technology into clinical practice.
In the future, the development of AIS QD@Cel represents a promising strategy for enhancing the therapeutic efficacy and safety of natural products, with the potential to revolutionize cancer treatment. However, further research is essential to address existing limitations and explore its broader clinical applications.
