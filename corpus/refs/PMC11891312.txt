## Introduction
The elimination of industrial wastewater, such as organic pollutants, especially methylene blue (MB), has always been a widely-held concern because of its toxicity and durability in water ecosystems[[CR1]]. Smart-engineered nanoparticles, however, propose innovative, eco-friendly solutions for water treatment applications, placing them at the forefront of growth in renewable and green technologies for wastewater cleanup[[CR2]],[[CR3]]. Among the materials used in photocatalysis, cadmium sulfide (CdS) is recognized for its capacity to harness visible light, resulting from an ideal band gap (2.2–2.4 eV), highlighting it as a standout candidate for enhanced capture of solar energy[[CR4]]. However, CdS poses some challenges: its sensitivity to photo-corrosion has limited its functional application[[CR4]]. Titanium dioxide (TiO2) is another popular semiconductor due to its chemical stability and reliable photocatalytic functionality[[CR5]],[[CR6]]. Nonetheless, its large band gap (3.2 eV) confines its response to absorbing only UV light, and its high rate of electron-hole recombination reduces its efficiency in solar-assisted systems[[CR7]],[[CR8]]. Coupling CdS with TiO2 has proven to be an innovative solution due to their synergistic effect: CdS extends optical absorption of TiO2 into the visible range, while TiO2 helps protect CdS from breaking down under light exposure, boosting the overall light-induced efficiency[[CR9]]–[[CR11]]. Despite the improvements achieved by pairing CdS with TiO2, the relatively low surface area and limited adsorption capacity hinder their practical implementation[[CR12]],[[CR13]]. Embedding different nanoparticles within stable, well-structured mesoporous materials, like MCM-41, has exhibited potential for boosting photocatalytic efficiency by leveraging their high surface area, networked channels, and strong adsorption properties, which collectively ensure better nanoparticle dispersion and pollutant-photocatalyst interaction[[CR12]],[[CR14]],[[CR15]]. While other studies have explored using mesoporous supports to increase CdS stability or enhance TiO2 solar energy performance[[CR9]]–[[CR11]],[[CR16]], the combined benefits achievable by integrating the CdS/TiO2/MCM-41 system have been overlooked. Furthermore, fabricating a well-dispersed CdS/TiO2 nanocomposite within a mesoporous matrix that harnesses the complementary effects of all three components while minimizing their drawbacks has yet to be achieved.
In this study, we aimed to identify the optimized conditions for the photocatalytic degradation of MB by building a library of CdS/TiO2/MCM-41 (CTM) composites employing varied loading ratios (w/w%) and carefully designed structures to boost photocatalytic activity and pollutant-nanoparticle interaction. Initially, the photocatalytic efficiency of pure CdS was enhanced by loading CdS/TiO2 (CT) nanoparticles onto MCM-41. Our strategy utilizes the high adsorption capacity and structural integrity of MCM-41 in conjunction with the improved optical properties of CdS–TiO2. This technique allows for uniform dispersion of CdS–TiO2 nanoparticles within a porous structure. Additionally, we determined the ideal CTM composition by comparing degradation efficiencies, repeated in triplicates, of different formulations of our library. Subsequently, since the process parameters affect efficiency[[CR17]], a set of experiments was designed using Design-Expert 11.1.0.1 Software (https://www.statease.com) to statistically study the effects of prominent factors, namely the pH of the solution, aeration rate, CdS/TiO2/MCM-41 concentration, MB concentration, and their interactions. Our research indicates that there is no theoretical model for synthesizing and applying this efficient photocatalyst in terms of organic dye destruction.
## Materials and methods
## Materials
Tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS, from Aldrich) was used as the silica source, and cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB, from Aldrich) as the cationic surfactant. Titanium butoxide (TBT), cadmium nitrate tetrahydrate, sodium sulfide nonahydrate, sodium hydroxide, ethanol, 25% ammonium hydroxide solution, hydrochloric acid, and methylene blue (MB) were all purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. All of them were used without any purification.
## Synthesis of samples
Mesoporous silica MCM-41 particles were synthesized using a modified sol-gel method[[CR18]]. In this process, a mixture of 0.6104 g of NaOH, 2.6 g of CTAB, and 10.245 g of TEOS in 1 l of deionized water was prepared. The solution was mixed vigorously (up to 5000 rpm) for 10 h at 80 °C. Then, the resulting particles were filtered, washed with deionized water, and dried at 100 °C for 8 h. Finally, the product was calcined in air at 550 °C to remove any residual CTAB, yielding the MCM-41 powder used in the following steps.
CdS nanoparticles were prepared using the chemical precipitation method[[CR19]]. First, 10 mL of 0.1 M Cd (NO₃)2 aqueous solution was added drop by drop to 100 mL of 0.1 M NaOH and mixed for 10 min at room temperature. Then, 10 mL of 0.1 M Na2S was quickly added and stirred for 10 min. The product was then separated by centrifugation at 7000 rpm, washed with deionized water, and dried at 60 °C for 8 h. A sol-gel method was used to prepare CdS/TiO2 (CT) nanocomposite[[CR10]]. 0.025 g of the CdS nanoparticles were dispersed in 100 mL of ethanol via sonication for 18 min. Then, 0.24 mL of ammonium hydroxide and 0.75 mL of TBT were added drop by drop to the solution, mixing continuously for 24 h at 45 °C. The resulting particles were filtered, washed with ethanol and deionized water several times, and dried at 70 °C for 8 h. CdS/TiO2/MCM-41 (CTM) nanocomposite was produced via a solid dispersion technique to create a library of different compositions[[CR20]]. Different weights of the CdS/TiO2 (CT) nanoparticles were suspended in ethanol by ultrasonication for 30 min. Then, different weights of MCM-41 were added to complete a library of various loading ratios (w/w%). The solutions were stirred for 2 h at room temperature and then dried at 70 °C for 8 h. The synthesis pathway of the mentioned samples is illustrated in Fig. 1.

## Experimental set-up
The activity of photocatalysts was assessed in a batch photoreactor (Fig. 2) containing MB solution (20 ppm, 300 ml) under visible irradiation from two 60 W lamps. The jacket kept maintain a constant temperature using a uniform cold-water flow. The temperature was regulated using a reactor jacket, and the reactor was shielded from ambient light. Various weights of each photocatalyst (0.1 g/L) were dispersed in the MB solution and mixed in the dark for 30 min to achieve adsorption-desorption equilibrium. Oxygen saturation was managed through continuous aeration by an air pump, as depicted in Fig. 2, because a consistent oxygen supply plays an essential role in producing reactive oxygen species necessary for the degradation of the dye. ## Results and discussion
## Characterization
XRD (X-ray diffraction) (Pw 1730 X-Ray Diffractometer, Bruker, Germany) was used to analyze the crystal structure of the samples using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) in the 2θ range from 15 to 85°. The XRD patterns of the samples are presented in Fig. 3a. Three distinct peaks at 26.68°, 44.04°, and 51.56° were observed for CdS nanoparticles, corresponding to d111, d220, and d311 Bragg reflections. These peaks align with the cubic structure of CdS (JCPDS No. 01-075-1546). The crystal grain size of CdS was estimated to be around 12.25 nm, calculated using the Scherrer equation. The formed titania, along with the CdS particles, has an amorphous structure, causing the suppression of the diffraction peaks of the CdS within the CT samples. For MCM-41, a broad peak at 22.9° was observed, which is attributed to amorphous SiO2 (JCPDS No. 0085-29), which remained unchanged in the XRD patterns of CTM samples, suggesting the stable incorporation of CT nanoparticles within the mesoporous structure of MCM-41[[CR22]]. Small-angle XRD patterns in Fig. 3b suggested a hexagonal mesoporous structure for MCM-41, indicated by a sharp d100 peak observed in both the unloaded and CT-loaded samples. The most intense peak was identified in the range of 1.7 to 2° and indexed to the d100 Bragg reflection as one of the mesoporous silica specifications[[CR22]]. This stability in microstructural integrity confirmed that the MCM-41 framework remains intact despite the CT loading. According to Fig. 3a, a decline in peak intensity was observed; however, as the CT load increased, the CTM pattern shifted to larger angles than that of pure MCM-41 and its width increased due to the reduction in its crystalline size.
Figure 4 demonstrates the FTIR spectra of MCM-41, CTM (5%), and CTM (15%). All samples exhibited comparable peaks at approximately 810 cm−1 and 1100 cm−1, along with a shoulder at 1220 cm−1[[CR23]]. These peaks relate to the symmetric stretching vibration of Si–O–Si structural units and the asymmetric vibration band of the tetrahedral SiO₄ units, respectively. A broad absorption band between 500 and 800 cm−1 and a weak peak around 1064 cm⁻1 were identified for CT nanoparticles, confirming the O–Ti–O networks and Cd-S bonds, respectively[[CR24]]–[[CR26]]. These absorption bands, however, overlapped with the characteristic silicon bonding of CTM samples. A consistent peak at ~ 1633 cm−1 was assigned to O–H bending vibrations of physiosorbed water, while a broad absorption band at 3382 cm−1 corresponds to surface hydroxyl groups, and O–H stretching vibrations[[CR27]].
High-resolution XPS spectra of Ti, Cd, S, Si, and O were collected to analyze the surface electronic states of CTM (15%) (Fig. 5a). Slightly shifted binding energy values were detected for all detected elements, possibly due to accumulating surface charge from electron emission[[CR28]]. The Ti 2p spectrum showed two clear peaks at 464.23 eV and 470.09 eV, related to Ti 2p₃/2 and Ti 2p₁/2, respectively (Fig. 5b))[[CR10]],[[CR16]]. These peaks indicated the presence of Ti⁴⁺ within CTM (15%). The Cd 3d spectrum displayed peaks at around 417.5 eV and 410.61 eV, assigned to Cd 3d₃/2 and Cd 3d₅/2, respectively (Fig. 5c)[[CR10]],[[CR11]],[[CR16]]. Characteristic peaks at 154.76 eV and 160.74 eV relate to S 2p₃/2 and S 2p₁/2, respectively, signifying sulfide elements (Fig. 5d)[[CR10]],[[CR11]],[[CR16]]. The absence of a disulfide peak in these results suggested the chemical stability of CdS within the composite. The O 1s spectrum presented peaks at 528.6 eV and 539.06 eV[[CR10]],[[CR11]],[[CR16]], attributed to surface Ti–O and Si–O bonds and adsorbed water molecules. There is also a peak at 109.4 eV in the Si 2p spectrum (Fig. 5e), indicating the presence of the silicate structure in CTM (15%)[[CR29]]. These findings highlight the successful integration of the components in CTM (15%).
The FESEM images of synthesized samples are illustrated in Fig. 6. The MCM-41 nanoparticles were spherical, with negligible agglomeration, indicating particle sizes ranging from 60 to 110 nm. These results validated the presence of MCM-41 particles with uniform surfaces, highlighting the homogeneous pores and walls characteristic of the mesoporous structure. For the CT particles, nanoparticles with an average size of around 25 nm were detected. The images of CTM samples signified that the homogeneous surface of MCM-41 remained intact after CT loading. The average particle sizes were approximately 95 and 99 nm for CTM (5%) and CTM (15%), respectively. Increased CT loading yielded larger CTM particle sizes associated with certain adherences of CT nanoparticles extending across the MCM-41 framework. The EDS analysis (Fig. 6 and Table S1), combined with SEM images, revealed that silicon (Si) and oxygen (O) were the main elements in MCM-41. At the same time, CT was composed of titanium (Ti), oxygen (O), cadmium (Cd), and sulfur (S). The element-based analysis of CTM samples unveiled the existence of Ti, Cd, and S, presenting explicit confirmation of CT integration with the MCM-41 framework. Silicon content reduction and titanium levels were observed as the CT loading increased from CTM (5%) to CTM (15%). This trend indicates a relative coverage of the MCM-41 framework with CT nanoparticles. The TEM results (Fig. 6e, f) showed clusters of well-ordered mesoporous structures with near-spherical shapes, relatively uniform surfaces, and a particle size of 98.34 nm, which was consistent with observations from the FESEM analysis. Slight clustering was monitored between particles, showing partial agglomeration; however, particle boundaries remained clear and distinct, with evidence of a small overlap or coalescence.
The specific surface area and porosity of samples were evaluated through the multipoint BET and BJH methods. Based on the IUPAC classification, the N2 adsorption-desorption isotherm (Fig. 7) of all samples exhibited Type IV hysteresis loops, representative of mesoporous materials[[CR30]]. The isothermal profiles of CTM and MCM-41 were similar, confirming that the structure of MCM-41 was maintained following the loading of CT nanoparticles. At low relative pressures (P/P₀ < 0.01), all samples displayed a linear increase associated with monolayer nitrogen adsorption onto the mesoporous walls. Subsequently, multilayer adsorption in the P/P₀ range of 0.01 to 0.3 occurred. A sharp increase in the isotherms between P/P₀ = 0.3 and 0.34 was ascribed to capillary condensation within the mesoporous channels. As the CT loading rose from 5 to 15%, however, the slope of this increase diminished, demonstrating a decline in pore diameter and volume resulting from both the penetration of CT particles into and their attachment onto the MCM-41 mesoporous framework.
The N2 adsorption-desorption data summary is presented in Table 4. CTM samples showed a smaller specific surface area and total pore volume compared to pure MCM-41, consistent with CT loading filling and blocking the mesopores of MCM-41. CTM samples, however, reached larger specific surface areas than CT samples, which follows the stated goal of expanding the reactive sites for the dye to interact and decompose. The BJH pore size distribution analysis (Fig. 7) confirmed the BET data, establishing that the structural integrity of MCM-41 sustained its stability while supporting the efficient incorporation of CT nanoparticles.

The PL intensity of samples was investigated to provide details on the charge carrier recombination characteristics of the samples, which directly affects the efficiency of photocatalysts. The excitation wavelength of 403 nm, within the visible light spectrum, was applied for all samples.
The PL spectrum of pure CdS, Fig. 8a, indicated the highest intensity among all samples, revealing that photogenerated electron-hole pairs had a significant recombination rate. The weak photocatalytic activity of pure CdS has been attributed to the high recombination rate observed here, as it substantially reduces the availability of charge carriers needed for catalytic reactions. The intensities were significantly reduced in CT samples compared to CdS. This reduction of intensities in CT samples presented the interaction between CdS and TiO2 compositions, which led to a lower recombination rate. It is proposed that TiO2 acts as an electron acceptor, transferring photogenerated electrons from the CdS conduction band to its conduction band, which suppresses recombination. A continued decrease in PL intensity was observed for CTM (5%) and CTM (15%) (Fig. 8a). This trend highlights the synergistic integration of CdS, TiO2, and the mesoporous MCM-41 material, suggesting efficient charge transfer between TiO2 and CdS in the composite. The further decline in PL intensity for CTM (5%) and CTM (15%) emphasizes the role of MCM-41 in improving the availability of active sites of CT nanoparticles, which facilitates interfacial interactions. Compared to CdS nanoparticles, this reduced recombination confirms a higher availability of photogenerated charge carriers for degradation processes. Figure 8b illustrates the UV-Vis DRS plot of CT, CTM (5%), and CTM (15%). The CTM (5%) sample showed higher reflectance than the others, indicating less interaction with light due to its lower CT content, leading to reduced light absorption. CTM (15%) displayed reflectance close to pure CT, indicating that the MCM-41 support has enhanced the dispersion of CT nanoparticles. The improved dispersion likely maximizes the effective surface area interacting with light, enhancing the photocatalytic potential of CTM (15%) even with fewer photoactive components due to better synergistic effects within the composite. Figure 9 presents the relative measure of MB photochemical degradation obtained by the synthesized composites compared to pure CdS under similar experimental conditions. All experiments were conducted in triplicates, and a significance level of 0.05 was implemented for statistical analysis. Pure CdS indicated the least efficiency (normalized to 1), which is consistent with its significant recombination rate, as reported in the PL spectra (Fig. 9). A significant enhancement in efficiency was detected when CdS was paired with TiO2, underscoring the advantage of charge separation via interface-mediated electron transfer between the two components, which also supports the PL spectra results (Fig. 9).
Adding MCM-41 as the underlying support for CT nanoparticles further amplified photodegradation efficiency, with CTM (15%) reaching a remarkable boost compared to pure CdS nanoparticles. The similar efficiency of CTM (5%) to CT samples, even with lower absorption observed in the DRS analysis (Fig. 8a), was attributed to as the greater dye adsorption/desorption in the solution. This performance is influenced by the remarkable mesoporous characteristics of the CTM samples compared to CT. This advancement indicates the role of MCM-41 in boosting the dispersion of CT nanoparticles with its porous structure, as illustrated by TEM (Fig. 6) and the BET results (Fig. 7). The porous characteristic of MCM-41 enhances the interface between pollutants and the photocatalytic reaction sites of the composite while also increasing light absorption and photocatalytic activity. Surprisingly, while CTM (20%) and CTM (25%) indicated slightly higher MB degradation, the differences were not statistically significant. These results imply that uncontrolled CT loading, despite increasing the percentage of photoactive components in the composite, may lead to partial pore clogging within the MCM-41 structure. This blockage can diminish the adsorption/desorption capacity of CTM composites and confine the reach of pollutants to the active sites, eventually interfering with the overall photocatalytic function of the composites. Our findings indicate that CTM (15%) provides the optimal balance between CT loading, nanoparticle dispersion, and photocatalytic activity. This data aligns with previous results, highlighting that the synergy among CdS, TiO2, and MCM-41 in the CTM (15%) composite significantly enhances photocatalytic efficiency while maintaining structural integrity and facilitating effective charge separation.

The impact of each factor on MB photodegradation processes was assessed systematically. pH (A) appeared as the most significant factor, with a coefficient of + 12.79 (Eq. 3), showing that increased basicity remarkably improves degradation efficiency. This increase in degradation is linked to the higher electrostatic interaction between the negatively charged composites and MB cations in high-pH environments. Validation of this observation was confirmed by the point of zero charges (pHpzc) of CTM (15%), determined using the batch equilibration technique[[CR17]] and found to be 5, implying that at pH > 5, the particles’ surface develops a negative charge. This negative surface charge improves the adsorption of cationic MB molecules, thus enabling an improved photocatalytic performance. Airflow (B), in comparison, indicated a coefficient of − 6.88, denoting that intense airflow decreases degradation efficiency, potentially because of truncated bubble retention durations and inefficient oxygen transfer to liquid pollutants. Finally, the MB-to-CTM ratio (C) exhibited a moderate impact, with a coefficient of + 3.07. These results underscore the significance of controlling an optimal catalyst-to-dye ratio to achieve the ideal photocatalytic performance. These findings present an integrated insight into the key parameters determining MB degradation efficiency using the selected nanoparticles CTM (15%).
The interactions between process parameters were significant, with pH (A) and the MB/CTM ratio (C) indicating a notable positive interaction (+ 1.21), as shown in 3D surface plots (Fig. 11a, b). These graphs exhibited the synergistic effect of controlling alkaline conditions with an adjusted catalyst-to-dye ratio and highlighted the importance of excessive airflow’s undesirable effects on pH impacts. Optimization findings, targeting a pH of 7 to attain industrial standards, determined the desired setup as pH = 7, airflow = 1.75 mL/min, and MB/CTM ratio = 110. This is illustrated in Fig. 11c and d, a heat map that shows a maximum degradation in the red. The model predicted a degradation of 44.61%, which was successfully achieved within the targeted conditions.

Also, the effective oxidizing agent of the best photocatalyst (CTM (15%)) was investigated under optimal conditions (according to Table 7). The degradation of MB was studied in the presence of different scavengers of •OH, e-, and h+ (methanol, ethanol, and sodium chloride, respectively). Considering the efficiency of MB degradation in the presence of each of the scavengers, the role of each active species in the degradation can be analyzed. The results show that without the scavenger, the degradation of MB using CTM (15%) was 45.6%, while by adding methanol, ethanol and sodium chloride, degradation efficiency was 33.8, 40.2 and 44.8%, respectively. As a result, it is evident that •OH plays a crucial role in MB degradation utilizing CTM (15%) as a photocatalyst, but no significant change was detected by adding other scavengers[[CR17]],[[CR34]],[[CR35]]. Additionally, mineralization of the target compound (MB) is the ultimate goal of photodegradation processes. TOC changes were investigated, where a significant decrease in TOC was observed during 90 min (63%), indicating effective mineralization of MB and its intermediates.
## Conclusion
A suitable procedure was applied to design and optimize an original CdS/TiO2/MCM-41 (CTM) photocatalyst for visible-light-induced methylene blue (MB) degradation, addressing environmental decontamination challenges. The investigation yielded high photocatalytic efficiency by merging the synergistic effects of CdS and TiO2 nanoparticles fusing with the porous structure of MCM-41. CTM (15%) was the most productive, providing balanced visible-light absorption, improved charge separation, and preserved mesoporous integrity. The particles were synthesized, characterized, and assessed using response surface methodology (RSM) with central composite design (CCD) refining parameters such as pH, airflow, and MB-to-CTM ratio. The second-order quadratic polynomial statistical model presented a strong 2 of 99.13%, demonstrating consistency. Among the parameters, pH is one of the most important ones, affecting the degradation process and influencing other factors, ranging from the number of oxidizing agents to wastewater concentration.
